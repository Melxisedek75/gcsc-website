## Section 1: State Summary & Regulatory Overview

### 1.1 Jurisdictional Character

New York is the highest-regulation state in the United States for financial services, operating alongside California as the most complex and expensive jurisdiction for market entry. The New York Department of Financial Services (DFS), headquartered in Manhattan with additional offices in Albany, exercises sweeping authority over banking, insurance, money transmission, virtual currency, and lending activities. No other state matches the breadth, depth, and enforcement intensity of New York's financial regulatory apparatus.

DFS Superintendent Adrienne A. Harris (appointed 2021) has presided over more than $177 million in penalties against virtual currency companies alone, cementing DFS's reputation as the nation's most aggressive crypto regulator. Her tenure has been marked by a clear policy position: the BitLicense framework will not be relaxed, and any entity engaging in virtual currency business activity with New York residents must obtain a license or face enforcement.

### 1.2 Three Licensing Regimes Relevant to SmartContractor

New York operates three distinct, non-overlapping licensing frameworks that collectively cover all SmartContractor product lines:

**Regime A — Licensed Lender Law (Banking Law Article 9, Sections 340-361):**  
Requires licensing for consumer loans of $25,000 or less and business/commercial loans of $50,000 or less when the lender charges, contracts for, or receives interest at rates greater than 16% APR. The 2018 amendments dramatically expanded the scope to include loan purchasers, arrangers, and facilitators — capturing marketplace lending models and fintech partnership structures that previously may have fallen outside the statute. Superintendent's Regulations Parts 401, 407, and 300 impose additional operational requirements. Penal Law Section 190.42 makes unlicensed lending a criminal offense.

**Regime B — BitLicense (23 NYCRR Part 200):**  
The nation's first comprehensive virtual currency regulatory framework, effective June 2015. Any Virtual Currency Business Activity involving New York or New York residents requires a BitLicense. Only approximately 30 companies have obtained a BitLicense since its inception — a strikingly low number that reflects the regime's notorious complexity, cost, and compliance burden. At least 10 cryptocurrency companies exited the New York market entirely when the BitLicense took effect. DFS has made clear that no exemptions exist for small companies, startups, or limited-activity models.

**Regime C — Money Transmitter License (Banking Law Article 13-B, Sections 640-663):**  
Required for fiat money transmission, entirely separate from the BitLicense. Applicants must post a $500,000 to $750,000 surety bond, maintain 100% permissible investments covering all outstanding transmission obligations, and endure a 12-18 month application timeline. This regime is relevant to any SmartContractor product involving the transmission, routing, or holding of customer funds — including claim advance proceeds and repayment flows.

## Section 3: Lending, Usury & Finance Licensing

### 3.1 Licensed Lender Law (Banking Law Article 9, Sections 340-361)

#### 3.1.1 Scope of Licensing Requirement

The Licensed Lender Law is one of the broadest and most aggressively enforced state lending statutes in the United States. Any person or entity engaging in the business of making covered loans without a license commits a criminal offense under Penal Law Section 190.42.

**Covered Loan Categories:**

| Loan Type | Principal Amount Threshold | Borrower Type |
|-----------|---------------------------|---------------|
| Consumer loans (personal, family, household, investment) | $25,000 or less | Individuals |
| Business and commercial loans | $50,000 or less | Businesses, including corporations, LLCs, partnerships |

**Rate Trigger:** The licensing requirement is triggered only when the lender charges, contracts for, or receives a rate of interest "greater than the lender would be permitted to charge if he were not a licensee" — meaning any rate above the 16% general usury ceiling.

**Key Statutory Language (Section 340):**

> "No person or other entity shall engage in the business of making loans in the principal amount of twenty-five thousand dollars or less for any loan to an individual for personal, family, household, or investment purposes and in a principal amount of fifty thousand dollars or less for business and commercial loans, and charge, contract for, or receive a greater rate of interest than the lender would be permitted by law to charge if he were not a licensee hereunder except as authorized by this article and without first obtaining a license from the superintendent."

#### 3.1.2 2018 Amendments — Critical Expansion

The 2018 budget legislation (effective in phases) fundamentally expanded Article 9's scope. These amendments must be understood as a deliberate legislative response to fintech marketplace lending models:

1. **Elimination of "within this state" requirement:** Out-of-state lenders who solicit or do business with New York borrowers are now explicitly covered, regardless of where the loan is technically originated or funded.

2. **Expansion of "engaging in the business of making loans":** The definition now explicitly includes:
   - Purchasing or otherwise acquiring loans or other forms of financing from others
   - Arranging or facilitating the funding of loans
   - Acting as an agent, broker, or intermediary for loan transactions

3. **Expansion of covered borrowers:** From "individuals" to "individuals and businesses located or doing business in this state"

4. **Expansion of covered lenders:** From natural persons to "persons and other entities," explicitly capturing corporations, LLCs, and other business forms

**Practical Impact:** A fintech platform that merely facilitates or arranges loans between third-party lenders and New York borrowers — without ever funding, holding, or servicing the loans — may still trigger the Article 9 licensing requirement. This directly impacts any SmartContractor marketplace or referral model.

#### 3.1.3 Penalties for Non-Compliance

| Violation | Statute | Penalty |
|-----------|---------|---------|
| Unlicensed lending | Penal Law Section 190.42 | Class E felony (criminal) |
| Civil usury (loans >16% APR, <$250K) | GOL Section 5-511 | Loan void; forfeiture of all interest; possible treble damages |
| Criminal usury (loans >25% APR) | Penal Law Section 190.40 | Class E felony; loan void per *Adar Bays* |
| Engaging in business without license | Banking Law Section 351 | Injunction, civil penalties, license denial |

#### 3.1.4 Application Process

| Requirement | Detail |
|-------------|--------|
| **Application fee** | $1,500 (non-refundable) |
| **Minimum net worth** | $250,000 (or $500,000 for certain applicants) |
| **Surety bond** | $10,000 minimum |
| **Background checks** | All principals, directors, officers |
| **Financial statements** | Audited financials required |
| **Business plan** | Required, with detailed lending methodology |
| **Timeline** | 6-12 months typical |
| **Renewal** | Annual, with fee and updated financials |

### 3.2 Usury Framework

New York's usury framework is among the strictest in the nation. The interplay between civil usury, criminal usury, licensed lender exemptions, and recent case law creates a complex compliance landscape.

#### 3.2.1 Civil Usury (16% APR)

- **Statutory basis:** General Obligations Law Section 5-501; Banking Law Section 14-a
- **Threshold:** 16% per annum for loans under $250,000
- **Effect:** Interest in excess of 16% is forfeited; the lender recovers only the principal
- **Exemptions:** Licensed lenders under Article 9 may charge rates above 16% (subject to their license terms); federally-insured banks may export rates under 12 U.S.C. Section 1831d

#### 3.2.2 Criminal Usury (25% APR)

- **Statutory basis:** Penal Law Section 190.40
- **Threshold:** 25% per annum
- **Effect:** Class E felony; loan is void under *Adar Bays*
- **No corporate exemption:** The landmark *Adar Bays, LLC v. GeneSYS ID, Inc.*, 37 N.Y.3d 320 (2021) ruling held that criminally usurious loans to corporate borrowers are void — overturning decades of lower court precedent that had protected corporate borrowers' usury defenses

#### 3.2.3 Usury Summary Matrix

| Loan Type | Under $250,000 | $250,000 — $2.5M | Over $2.5M |
|-----------|---------------|-------------------|------------|
| Individual loans | 16% civil usury applies; 25% criminal usury applies | Civil usury exempt; criminal usury (25%) still applies | Fully exempt |
| Corp/LLC loans (general) | No civil usury; 25% criminal usury applies | No civil usury; 25% criminal usury applies | Fully exempt |
| Corp/LLC + 1-2 family dwelling (<6 months) | 16% civil; 25% criminal (special rule) | No civil usury; 25% criminal | Fully exempt |

**Critical Note:** The *Adar Bays* decision eliminated any safe harbor for lending to corporate borrowers at rates between 16% and 25%. While civil usury penalties may not apply, any loan exceeding 25% APR to any borrower — corporate or individual — is criminal usury and void.

### 3.3 Commercial Financing Disclosure Law

New York enacted a commercial financing disclosure law requiring specific "cost of credit" disclosures for commercial financing transactions of $2.5 million or less. This applies independently of the licensing requirements:

- **Covered providers:** Any person who extends a specific offer of commercial financing
- **Required disclosures:** APR, finance charge, total repayment amount, term, payment frequency, any prepayment penalties
- **Format:** Standardized format required
- **Penalties:** $2,000 per violation; private right of action with actual damages, attorney's fees, and punitive damages for willful violations

### 3.4 Proposed SB 1450 (2023 Session)

Although SB 1450 did not pass as of the research date, it signals important regulatory direction:

- Would require licensing for "commercial financing products" of $500,000 or less
- Covers loans, asset-based financing, leasing, and factoring
- Would void unlicensed financing (making it legally uncollectible)
- Represents a clear legislative intent to expand oversight of commercial finance

### 3.5 Bank Partnership / True Lender Considerations

- **12 U.S.C. Section 1831d:** Allows FDIC-insured state banks to export their home-state interest rates, including into New York
- **DFS Position:** DFS has acknowledged preemption for out-of-state, federally-insured banks (2011 Interpretation Letter)
- **"True Lender" Doctrine:** DFS examines the substance, not the form, of lending arrangements. If a fintech platform is the true lender (determines credit risk, markets to borrowers, services loans), the bank partnership model will not shield the platform from Article 9 licensing
- **Heightened Scrutiny:** New York has been among the most aggressive states in challenging fintech-bank partnership models, particularly where the bank's role is ministerial and the fintech retains the economic risk

| Equipment credit to contractor, >$50,000, <=16% APR | No (amount above threshold) | Compliant if rate <=16% |

## Section 4: Money Transmission, Escrow & Custody Requirements

> **This is a NEW section added to address the specific regulatory requirements for money transmission, escrow activities, and custodial arrangements in New York — all critical to SmartContractor's operational model for both product lines.**

### 4.1 Money Transmitter License (Banking Law Article 13-B, Sections 640-663)

#### 4.1.1 Scope and Trigger

New York requires a Money Transmitter License for any person or entity engaged in the business of "selling, issuing or transmitting" checks, drafts, money orders, or other instruments for the transmission or payment of money, or the "receiving of money for transmission."

**Key Statutory Language (Section 641):**

> "No person shall engage in the business of selling, issuing or transmitting checks, drafts or money orders, or of receiving money for transmission, in this state, or engage in such business outside this state and solicit, advertise or accept customers located in this state, unless such person is licensed by the superintendent."

#### 4.1.2 Activities Requiring a Money Transmitter License

The following SmartContractor activities may trigger Money Transmitter License requirements:

| Activity | MTL Triggered? | Analysis |
|----------|---------------|----------|
| Receiving claim advance funds for transmission to contractor | **YES** | Receiving money for transmission |
| Routing repayment from insurance proceeds to SmartContractor | **YES** | Transmitting money on behalf of others |
| Holding customer funds in escrow or suspense pending claim resolution | **YES** | Storing monetary value for others |
| Facilitating payment from homeowner to contractor | **YES** | Money transmission intermediary |
| Token-to-fiat conversion and disbursement | **YES** | Transmitting fiat currency |
| Simple referral to licensed third-party transmitter | **NO** | No handling of funds |

#### 4.1.3 Money Transmitter Application Requirements

| Requirement | Detail |
|-------------|--------|
| **Minimum net worth** | $500,000 |
| **Surety bond** | $500,000 — $750,000 (determined by superintendent based on volume) |
| **Permissible investments** | 100% of aggregate transmission obligations must be maintained in permissible investments (cash, government securities, FDIC-insured deposits) |
| **Application fee** | $5,000 (non-refundable) |
| **Background checks** | All principals, executive officers, directors, 10%+ shareholders |
| **Financial statements** | Audited, prepared by independent CPA |
| **Compliance program** | Required — AML/BSA, OFAC, fraud prevention |
| **Timeline** | 12-18 months typical (DFS is thorough and may request multiple rounds of supplemental information) |
| **Renewal** | Annual; $2,500 renewal fee plus updated financials |

#### 4.1.4 Distinction from BitLicense

The Money Transmitter License and BitLicense are **entirely separate** and do not overlap in scope:

| Aspect | Money Transmitter License | BitLicense |
|--------|--------------------------|------------|
| **Regulated activity** | Fiat currency transmission | Virtual currency business activity |
| **Statutory basis** | Banking Law Article 13-B | 23 NYCRR Part 200 |
| **Surety bond** | $500K — $750K | $500K minimum (similar) |
| **Permissible investments** | 100% required | Similar custody requirements |
| **Cybersecurity (Part 500)** | Yes | Yes |
| **Can one substitute for the other?** | **NO** | **NO** |

**Critical Implication:** If SmartContractor handles both fiat currency (claim proceeds, loan disbursements, repayments) and virtual currency (token collateral), it would need **both licenses** to operate legally in New York. Obtaining one does not satisfy the requirements of the other.

### 4.2 Escrow Regulation in New York

#### 4.2.1 Escrow Activities Requiring Regulation

New York does not have a standalone "escrow agent" license in the manner of some states (e.g., California). However, escrow activities are regulated through multiple overlapping frameworks:

1. **Banking Law Article 13-B (Money Transmitter):** If escrow involves receiving money for transmission or holding funds on behalf of others, a Money Transmitter License may be required.

2. **Banking Law Article 9 (Licensed Lender):** If escrow is incidental to licensed lending activities, it may be covered under the Licensed Lender License.

3. **Insurance Law:** Escrow of insurance claim proceeds may be subject to DFS oversight as part of insurance claims handling practices.

4. **Real Property Law:** Escrow in real estate transactions has specific statutory requirements (Section 265-a), though this applies primarily to residential real property.

#### 4.2.2 DFS Supervisory Expectations for Escrow

While not codified in a single escrow statute, DFS has established clear supervisory expectations for the handling of customer funds:

- **Segregation:** Customer funds must be segregated from operational funds at all times
- **Interest-bearing accounts:** Where customer funds are held for extended periods, DFS has urged servicers to maintain them in interest-bearing escrow accounts (see DFS Sandy Best Practices, 2013)
- **Recordkeeping:** Complete transactional records must be maintained, with daily reconciliation
- **No commingling:** Customer funds may not be used for operational expenses, investments, or any purpose other than the designated transfer
- **Documentation:** Complete documentation must accompany all disbursements

#### 4.2.3 SmartContractor Escrow Analysis

| Escrow Scenario | Regulatory Treatment | License Required? |
|-----------------|---------------------|-------------------|
| Holding claim advance funds pending contractor completion | Money transmission / custody | **MTL likely required** |
| Holding progress payments for home improvement work | Licensed lender + MTL analysis | **Yes — Article 9 and/or MTL** |
| Escrow of token collateral (virtual currency) | BitLicense activity | **BitLicense required** |
| Holding insurance proceeds for mortgagee disbursement | Covered by servicer regulations | Subject to servicer/mortgagee rules |
| Simple escrow facilitation via licensed third party | Agency arrangement | No (if properly structured) |

### 4.3 Custody Requirements for Customer Assets

#### 4.3.1 DFS Custody Guidance (January 2023, Updated September 2025)

DFS has issued the most detailed virtual currency custody guidance of any U.S. regulator. The guidance applies to all DFS-regulated Virtual Currency Entities (VCEs), including BitLicense holders:

**Core Custody Principles:**

| Principle | Requirement | Impact on SmartContractor |
|-----------|-------------|---------------------------|
| **Segregation** | Customer assets must be segregated from corporate assets at all times | SmartContractor cannot commingle customer tokens with operational funds |
| **Beneficial interest** | Beneficial interest in customer assets must always remain with the customer | Tokens held as "collateral" must not transfer ownership |
| **No debtor-creditor relationship** | VCE custodians cannot establish a debtor-creditor relationship when providing custody | Lending against collateral may create such a relationship — structural tension |
| **No encumbrance** | Customer VC **cannot** be used as security for obligations of the custodian (23 NYCRR 200.9(c)) | **Directly conflicts with token collateral model** |
| **Sub-custody** | Any sub-custody arrangement requires DFS prior written approval | Third-party custodian relationships require pre-approval |
| **Disclosure** | Clear written disclosure of custodial (not debt) relationship required | Must explicitly state that custody is not a deposit account |

#### 4.3.2 September 2025 Updated Guidance — Sub-Custody Framework

The September 2025 updated guidance introduced additional requirements for sub-custody arrangements:

- **Prior DFS approval required** before entering any sub-custody arrangement
- **Due diligence:** Licensee must conduct and document comprehensive due diligence on any sub-custodian
- **Agreement requirements:** Written agreement must specify segregation, access controls, and reporting obligations
- **Ongoing monitoring:** Continuous monitoring of sub-custodian's compliance and financial condition
- **No discharge of liability:** Use of sub-custodian does not discharge licensee's regulatory obligations
- **Reporting:** Annual reporting of all sub-custody arrangements to DFS

#### 4.3.3 Fiat Currency Custody (For Money Transmitters)

Money transmitters must maintain:
- **100% permissible investments** equal to or greater than outstanding transmission obligations at all times
- **Permissible investments** include: cash, deposits in FDIC-insured banks, U.S. government securities, certain high-grade corporate obligations
- **Daily reconciliation** of transmission obligations against permissible investments
- **Quarterly reporting** to DFS on permissible investments
- **No investment of customer funds** in non-permissible assets

### 4.4 Practical Compliance Framework for SmartContractor

Given the overlapping requirements, any New York operation handling customer funds or assets would need to implement:

| Layer | Requirement | Timeline |
|-------|-------------|----------|
| 1. Money Transmitter License | For fiat currency handling | 12-18 months |
| 2. BitLicense | For virtual currency handling | 12-18 months (parallel) |
| 3. Licensed Lender License | For lending >16% APR at covered amounts | 6-12 months |
| 4. Custody Framework | Segregation, reconciliation, reporting | Ongoing |
| 5. Cybersecurity (23 NYCRR 500) | CISO, risk assessment, testing | Ongoing |
| 6. AML/BSA Program | OFAC, SARs, customer verification | Ongoing |

**Verdict:** The cumulative licensing burden for a full SmartContractor operation in New York — handling both fiat and virtual currency, making loans, and holding collateral — would require at minimum three separate DFS licenses (MTL, BitLicense, Licensed Lender), each with its own application timeline, bonding requirements, net worth thresholds, and ongoing compliance obligations. The estimated combined timeline for all three licenses is 18-36 months with no guarantee of approval. This is among the most burdensome regulatory frameworks in any U.S. state.

## Section 5: Token Collateral, Cryptocurrency & BitLicense

### 5.1 BitLicense Regime (23 NYCRR Part 200) — The Strictest in the United States

#### 5.1.1 Historical Context

The BitLicense was promulgated by DFS in June 2015, making New York the first state to create a comprehensive regulatory framework for virtual currency business activity. The regime was crafted by then-Superintendent Benjamin Lawsky and has remained substantially unchanged despite widespread industry criticism and multiple superintendent transitions.

Since 2015, approximately 30 companies have obtained BitLicenses. At least 10 prominent cryptocurrency companies — including Bitfinex, Kraken (initially), and others — exited the New York market entirely rather than seek licensure. The BitLicense application fee alone is $5,000 (non-refundable), but total application costs including legal, compliance infrastructure, and consulting typically exceed $500,000 to $1,000,000.

#### 5.1.2 Virtual Currency Business Activity Definition

Pursuant to 23 NYCRR Section 200.2(q), "Virtual Currency Business Activity" means any one of the following:

| Activity # | Description | SmartContractor Relevance |
|------------|-------------|--------------------------|
| 1 | Receiving Virtual Currency for Transmission or Transmitting Virtual Currency | Moderate — if tokens are transferred between wallets |
| 2 | Storing, Holding, or Maintaining Custody or Control of Virtual Currency on Behalf of Others | **HIGH** — token collateral lockup directly triggers this activity |
| 3 | Buying and Selling Virtual Currency as a Customer Business | Low — not a core SmartContractor activity |
| 4 | Performing Exchange Services as a Customer Business | Moderate — if token-to-fiat conversion is offered |

**Key Statutory Language:**

> "No person shall engage in any Virtual Currency Business Activity involving New York or a New York Resident unless such person has obtained a license (a 'BitLicense') from the superintendent."

**Critical Note:** Only **one** of the five enumerated activities needs to be present to trigger the BitLicense requirement. SmartContractor's token collateral model squarely triggers Activity #2 (storing/holding virtual currency on behalf of others).

#### 5.1.4 Key Prohibitions Affecting SmartContractor

**Section 200.3(b) — Unlicensed Agents Prohibited:**

**Section 200.9(c) — Customer Asset Protection:**  
This provision is the single greatest structural obstacle to SmartContractor's token collateral model:

> "[A BitLicensee] is prohibited from selling, transferring, assigning, lending, hypothecating, pledging, or otherwise using or encumbering [customer virtual currency] assets."

**Analysis:** SmartContractor's token collateral model fundamentally depends on holding customer tokens as security for a loan obligation — precisely the activity that Section 200.9(c) prohibits. Even with a BitLicense, a licensee cannot use customer virtual currency as collateral for its own obligations. This creates an irreconcilable structural conflict between the BitLicense regulatory framework and the secured lending model using virtual currency collateral.

#### 5.2.2 The Martin Act Factor

## Section 6: Insurance Claim Advances & Claim Proceeds Financing

### 6.1 Emergency Claim Payment Framework

#### 6.1.1 Statutory Requirements

New York **does not have a specific statute** requiring insurers to pay emergency claim advances on property insurance claims. Unlike some states that mandate advance payments for emergency repairs or additional living expenses, New York relies on:

1. **Policy terms and conditions**
2. **General good faith and fair dealing requirements**
3. **DFS supervisory expectations** (non-binding but influential)
4. **Industry standard practices**

#### 6.1.2 DFS Superstorm Sandy Best Practices (February 2013)

Following Superstorm Sandy, DFS issued supervisory guidance urging banks and mortgage servicers to expedite the release of insurance claim funds. These "best practices" represent DFS's supervisory expectations:

| Best Practice | Description | Binding? |
|-------------|-------------|----------|
| Immediate release of emergency/advance funds | Banks/servicers should release funds designated "emergency" or "advance" immediately | No — supervisory guidance |
| Minimize documentation | Reduce documentation requirements for emergency disbursements | No — supervisory guidance |
| Interest-bearing escrow | Hold insurance proceeds in interest-bearing accounts | No — supervisory guidance |
| Same-day processing | Process all mail day of receipt | No — supervisory guidance |
| Same-day release | Release proceeds day of receipt (with complete documentation) | No — supervisory guidance |
| Rapid inspection deployment | Deploy inspectors within 2 business days | No — supervisory guidance |
| Servicer-funded inspections | Conduct inspections at servicer's expense | No — supervisory guidance |

**Important:** While these are not legally binding regulations, DFS may consider a servicer's compliance with these best practices in any enforcement action or examination. They represent the superintendent's view of appropriate conduct.

#### 6.1.3 Additional Living Expenses (ALE)

ALE coverage (also called "Loss of Use") is policy-dependent:

- Standard HO-3 homeowners policies include ALE coverage when the dwelling is rendered uninhabitable by a covered peril
- Coverage limits are typically a percentage of the dwelling coverage (e.g., 20-30%)
- ALE covers: temporary housing, meals, storage, transportation, and other reasonable increases in living expenses
- ALE does **not** cover the cost of repairing the dwelling itself
- Documentation requirements vary by insurer but generally require receipts and proof of uninhabitability

#### 6.1.4 Timely Claim Payment

- **No-fault motor vehicle claims:** Insurance Law Section 5106 requires payment within 30 days of proof of claim
- **Property claims:** No specific statutory deadline; governed by policy terms and general good faith requirements
- **Unfair claims settlement practices:** Pattern or practice of unreasonable delay may constitute unfair claim practice under DFS regulations

### 6.2 Claim Proceeds and Mortgage Holder Rights

#### 6.2.1 Standard Mortgage Clause

The standard mortgage clause (also called "loss payable clause") is universally included in homeowners insurance policies where a mortgage exists:

- **Mortgagee named as loss payee:** The lender/mortgagee is named as an additional insured with respect to the mortgaged property
- **Payment to mortgagee:** Claim payments for covered losses to the mortgaged property are made jointly payable to the homeowner and mortgagee, or directly to the mortgagee in certain circumstances
- **Mortgagee's independent rights:** The mortgagee has independent rights under the policy — the insurer must provide 10-day notice of cancellation to the mortgagee, and the mortgagee's interest is protected even if the insured violates policy terms
- **Order of precedence:** Loss payments are applied "to the extent of [the mortgagee's] interest and in the order of precedence"

#### 6.2.2 Loss Draft Handling by Servicers

Mortgage servicers have developed standardized procedures for handling insurance loss drafts:

| Scenario | Servicer Action |
|----------|----------------|
| Claim <$40K and loan current | May endorse check to borrower; may require contractor estimate |
| Claim >$40K or loan delinquent | Monitored claim — funds held in escrow, disbursed in stages |
| Monitored claim | Inspector verifies completion at each stage before next disbursement |
| Final payment | Released upon certificate of completion and final inspection |
| Emergency advance | May be released immediately for emergency repairs (varies by servicer) |

### 6.3 Claim Financing / Claim Advance Products

#### 6.3.1 Product Structure Analysis

SmartContractor's ClaimBridge product — providing advances against expected insurance claim proceeds — must be analyzed under multiple New York legal frameworks:

| Framework | Trigger | Risk Level |
|-----------|---------|------------|
| Article 9 Licensed Lender | If advance is a "loan" <=$25K (consumer) or <=$50K (business) at >16% APR | **HIGH** |
| Martin Act (securities) | If advance is structured as an investment contract or security | **HIGH** |
| Money Transmitter | If SmartContractor receives/transmits claim funds | **HIGH** |
| Public Adjuster Law | If SmartContractor negotiates or facilitates claim negotiation | **HIGH** |
| Assignment of Benefits | If advance is repaid via assignment of claim proceeds | **UNKNOWN** |

#### 6.3.2 Martin Act Securities Risk

The Martin Act creates significant risk for any novel claim-financing product:

- If claim advances are structured such that the "investor" (the party providing advance funds) expects a return derived from the efforts of others (the claims process), the product may constitute an "investment contract" and thus a "security" under the Martin Act
- The NY Attorney General has demonstrated willingness to apply the Martin Act to non-traditional financial products (see crypto lending enforcement)
- No safe harbor exists for "commercial" or "business purpose" transactions — the Martin Act applies broadly

DFS actively monitors servicer and insurer conduct following major disasters:

### 7.1 AOB Status in New York: UNKNOWN — REQUIRES COUNSEL REVIEW

#### 7.1.1 No Comprehensive AOB Statute for Property Claims

Research did not identify a comprehensive Assignment of Benefits statute governing property insurance claims in New York. This stands in marked contrast to states like Florida (which has detailed AOB statutes with specific requirements for font size, cancellation windows, and post-loss limitations) or Texas (which has specific AOB legislation for certain lines).

The absence of a specific statute does **not** mean AOBs are freely permitted. Rather, it means the regulatory status of property insurance AOBs in New York is governed by:

1. **General contract assignment principles** (common law)
2. **Insurance policy terms and conditions**
3. **Insurance Law provisions** that may indirectly affect AOB validity
4. **DFS supervisory discretion**

#### 7.1.2 DFS Opinion on AOB (Health Insurance Context)

DFS Opinion 00-11-06 addressed assignment of benefits in the health insurance context:

> "Neither the New York Insurance Law, nor the regulations promulgated thereunder, obligates an insurer to honor an assignment of benefits request."

**Important Limitation:** This opinion was issued in the health insurance context and does not directly govern property insurance claims. However, it demonstrates DFS's general posture: insurers are not compelled to honor AOB requests unless the policy or a specific statute requires it.

#### 7.1.3 General Contract Assignment Principles

Under New York common law:

- **Assignments are generally permitted** unless prohibited by statute, contract, or public policy
- **Anti-assignment clauses** in insurance policies are generally enforceable in New York
- **Notice to obligor:** The assignee should give notice of the assignment to the obligor (the insurer)
- **Defenses:** The obligor may raise against the assignee any defenses that the obligor had against the assignor

#### 7.1.4 Potential Policy Barriers

New York requires all public adjusters to be licensed by DFS. The licensing requirements are comprehensive and strictly enforced:

| GCSC or any GCSC employee/contractor | **YES — if negotiating claims** | Same as above |

### 8.2 Critical Statutory Restrictions

#### 8.2.1 Core Prohibition

Insurance Law Section 2108(a)(3):

> "No adjuster shall act on behalf of an insurer unless licensed as an independent adjuster, and no adjuster shall act on behalf of an insured unless licensed as a public adjuster."

This is an absolute prohibition. There are no exceptions for "assistance," "coaching," or "facilitation" — if an unlicensed person acts on behalf of an insured in negotiations with an insurance company, they violate the statute.

| Must use Standard Form 1 compensation agreement | Regulation | License discipline; agreement may be void |
| Fee cap: 12.5% standard, 20% supplemental | Section 2108(p) | Excess fees must be refunded; license discipline |

### 8.3 GCSC Implications — Absolute Prohibition
