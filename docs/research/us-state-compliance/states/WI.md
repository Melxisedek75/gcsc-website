## Section 1 — Executive Summary & State Overview

Wisconsin presents a **HIGH REGULATION** environment for SmartContractor/GCSC product deployment. The state combines aggressive consumer lending restrictions — including a constitutional general usury cap of **8.75%**, one of the lowest in the nation — with newly enacted insurance claim restrictions that fundamentally reshape the landscape for contractor-claim interactions. The passage of **2025 Wis. Act 230 (SB 531)**, creating Wis. Stat. § 632.11 effective **December 1, 2026**, introduces comprehensive regulation of Assignment of Benefits (AOB) agreements for residential contractors, while the landmark *In re Joshua Lesley* decision (OCI 2025) confirms that contractors who negotiate insurance claims risk being found to be unlicensed public adjusters. Wisconsin is one of the most challenging states for GCSC deployment, with substantial legal barriers across lending, insurance, and contractor regulation domains.

### Why Wisconsin Matters for GCSC

- **Severe Weather Exposure**: Wisconsin experiences significant hailstorms, tornadoes, winter storms, and flooding that generate substantial property insurance claims annually. The state ranks among the top Midwestern states for hail damage frequency.
- **Active Contractor Market**: Wisconsin has a robust residential construction and restoration contractor market, particularly in the Milwaukee, Madison, Green Bay, and Fox Valley metro areas.
- **Low Usury Ceiling Creates Pricing Pressure**: The 8.75% general usury cap severely constrains pricing flexibility for any consumer-facing loan product.
- **Aggressive Public Adjuster Enforcement**: OCI actively pursues contractors who engage in claim negotiation, as demonstrated by the *In re Joshua Lesley* case.
- **New AOB Law Creates Uncertainty**: The first-of-its-kind Wisconsin AOB statute for residential contractors takes effect December 1, 2026, creating a complex compliance framework with unknown practical effects.

### Five Critical Regulatory Barriers

**1. General Usury Cap of 8.75%.** Article XV, Section 21 of the Wisconsin Constitution limits interest rates to 8.75% per annum unless a licensed lender exception applies. This is among the lowest usury ceilings in the United States and creates a fundamental economic barrier to unlicensed consumer lending.

**2. Licensed Lender Requirement for Loans >18% APR.** Under Wis. Stat. § 138.09, any finance charge exceeding 18% APR on a consumer loan of $25,000 or less requires a loan company license from DFI. 2023 Wis. Act 267 expanded this to cover purchasers and servicers of such loans.

**3. 2025 Wis. Act 230 (SB 531) — New AOB Restrictions.** Effective December 1, 2026, § 632.11 heavily regulates AOB contracts for residential contractors: prohibits contractors from waiving deductibles, prohibits contractors from representing insureds or negotiating with insurers, voids non-compliant contracts, and imposes penalties up to $5,000 per violation. This statute fundamentally alters the contractor-claim relationship.

**4. *In re Joshua Lesley* — Contractors as Unlicensed Public Adjusters.** The OCI 2025 decision confirmed that contractors who combine repair work with claim-handling functions (submitting estimates, negotiating with insurers) are illegally acting as unlicensed public adjusters. Status is determined by **conduct, not title**, creating significant risk for any contractor-claim interaction.

**5. Public Adjuster AOB Prohibition.** Under Wis. Stat. § 629.07, public adjusters cannot request or accept assignments of insurance policy proceeds under any circumstances. This eliminates a potential pathway for claim financing through licensed public adjuster channels.

## Section 3 — Lending, Finance & Usury Framework

### 3.1 The 8.75% Constitutional Usury Ceiling

The foundation of Wisconsin lending law is **Article XV, Section 21 of the Wisconsin Constitution**, which limits the rate of interest on any loan to **8.75% per annum** unless an exception applies. This is one of the lowest constitutional usury caps in the United States and creates a fundamental economic constraint on unlicensed lending.

> **Wis. Const. Art. XV, § 21:** "The legislature shall provide by law that the rate of interest shall not exceed eight and three-fourths percent per annum."

**Consequences of Usury in Wisconsin:**
- The usurious interest is **forfeited entirely** — the lender cannot recover any interest charged in excess of the lawful rate
- The borrower may recover **twice the amount of interest paid** as a penalty (Wis. Stat. § 138.06)
- Usury is a **defense to collection** and may be raised at any time
- **No exemption for out-of-state lenders** — Wisconsin usury law applies to any loan made to a Wisconsin resident

**Key Exceptions to the 8.75% Ceiling:**

| Exception | Statute | Maximum Rate | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| DFI Licensed Lender | Wis. Stat. § 138.09 | No statutory maximum for licensed lenders | License required; NMLS application; $500 annual fee + $300 investigation fee |
| Commercial/Business-Purpose Loans | Wis. Stat. § 138.09(1m) | No maximum for bona fide business loans | Must be properly documented; substance-over-form analysis applies |
| Credit Union Loans | Various federal/state statutes | As permitted by charter | Federally chartered CUs preempt state usury |
| National Banks | 12 U.S.C. § 85 | Federal exportation | National banks may charge interest at rate permitted by their home state |

**Impact on GCSC:**

The 8.75% usury cap severely constrains GCSC's pricing flexibility in Wisconsin. If GCSC cannot obtain a § 138.09 license, **any loan product must be priced at or below 8.75% APR** to avoid usury violations — which is likely not economically viable given credit risk and operational costs. The licensed lender path provides rate flexibility but requires DFI licensing through NMLS.

### 3.2 Licensed Lender Requirements (Wis. Stat. § 138.09)

Any entity charging a finance charge **exceeding 18% per year** on a Wisconsin consumer loan of **$25,000 or less** must obtain a **loan company license** from DFI.

#### License Requirement

> **"No person may engage in the business of making loans of $25,000 or less to any borrower at a rate of interest greater than 18% per year without first having obtained a license from the [DFI]."** — Wis. Stat. § 138.09(1m)(a)

| Threshold | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Loan Amount** | $25,000 or less |
| **Borrower Type** | Individual natural person (consumer loan) |
| **Interest Rate** | Exceeds 18% per annum |
| **License** | Loan Company License |
| **Regulator** | Wisconsin DFI — Division of Banking |

#### Licensing Requirements

| Requirement | Detail | Statute |
|---|---|---|
| **Application** | Through NMLS | § 138.09(1m) |
| **Annual License Fee** | $500 | § 138.09(1m) |
| **Investigation Fee** | $300 | § 138.09(1m) |
| **Maximum Rate** | No statutory cap for licensed lenders | Current law |
| **Examination** | DFI may examine books and records | § 138.09 |

**Proposed SB 759** (introduced December 2025, not yet enacted) would impose a **36% APR cap** on nonbank consumer loans and codify "true lender" tests. GCSC must monitor this legislation.

#### "True Lender" Expansion (2023 Wis. Act 267 / SB 668)

Effective 2025, the Licensed Lenders Law was expanded to cover:
- Any person that **purchases** a consumer loan with APR >18%, even if originally made by an exempt entity (e.g., bank)
- **Servicers** that directly collect payments from or enforce rights against customers
- **Exemption** exists for securitization/investment transactions if loans are serviced by a licensee
- This expansion creates potential licensing exposure for GCSC if it purchases or services loans

### 3.3 Wisconsin Consumer Act (Wis. Stat. Chs. 421–427)

The Wisconsin Consumer Act (WCA) provides robust consumer protections for consumer credit transactions:

- Applies to consumer credit transactions of **$25,000 or less** not secured by first-lien real estate mortgage
- Requires specific **truth-in-lending disclosures** (§ 422.303)
- Prohibits deceptive advertising of credit terms (§ 423.301)
- Governs finance charges, delinquency charges, deferral charges
- Provides consumer **right to cure defaults** before acceleration
- Incorporates into every consumer loan by operation of law
- Prohibits unconscionable practices (§ 425.301)

**Penalty for WCA Violations:**
- Actual damages
- Statutory penalties
- Attorney fees
- Potential class action exposure

### 3.4 Commercial / Business-Purpose Loans

Business-purpose loans are generally **exempt** from the Wisconsin Consumer Act and the 8.75% usury cap:

| Factor | Business-Purpose Loan | Consumer Loan |
|---|---|---|
| Borrower | LLC, Corporation, Partnership, or sole proprietor with clear business purpose | Individual natural person |
| Purpose | Working capital, equipment, inventory for business | Personal, family, household |
| License Required | Generally NO (if bona fide business purpose) | YES (if ≤$25K and >18%) |
| Usury Limit | No maximum | 8.75% (unlicensed); no cap (licensed) |
| Documentation | Commercial loan agreement, business purpose certification, financial statements | Consumer disclosures, WCA compliance |

**Critical Caution:** Wisconsin courts examine the **true nature and substance** of the transaction. A loan to a newly formed LLC with no business history, where the funds are used for residential roof repair, may be recharacterized as a consumer loan triggering the 8.75% usury cap and WCA requirements. Wisconsin counsel must review all loan structures.

### 3.5 Loan Brokering / Arranging

Under § 138.09, "business" includes **brokering, arranging, or facilitating loans**. SB 759, if enacted, would expand "true lender" analysis to predominant economic interest and totality of circumstances. Any entity that assists in arranging loans exceeding 18% APR on consumer loans ≤$25,000 may trigger licensed lender requirements.

## Section 4 — Escrow, Money Transmitter & Consumer Protection (NEW)

### 4.1 Escrow Regulation in Wisconsin

Escrow activities in Wisconsin fall within the potential regulatory jurisdiction of the **Wisconsin Department of Financial Institutions (DFI)**. While Wisconsin does not have a standalone "escrow agent" statute for general business transactions, entities performing escrow-like functions may be subject to DFI oversight depending on the transaction type.

#### 4.1.1 DFI Escrow Authority

The DFI has broad authority under Wis. Stat. Chapter 217 and general financial services statutes to regulate escrow activities:

| Governing Authority | Statute/Rule | Applicability |
|---|---|---|
| Wisconsin DFI — Division of Banking | Wis. Stat. Ch. 217 (Money Transmitter) | Escrow activities involving money transmission or currency exchange |
| Wisconsin DFI — Licensed Lender | Wis. Stat. § 138.09 | Escrow of loan proceeds by licensed lenders |
| Wisconsin OCI | Wis. Stat. Ch. 600–646 (Insurance Code) | Escrow of insurance claim proceeds; loss draft processing |
| Wisconsin DSPS | Wis. Admin. Code ch. ATCP 110 | Home improvement contract deposits and trade practices |

**Key Insight:** Wisconsin law does not have a specific escrow licensing statute comparable to Texas or Florida. However, DFI has broad interpretive authority under Chapter 217 to regulate escrow activities that involve money transmission, and licensed lenders holding loan funds in escrow must comply with DFI requirements.

#### 4.1.2 Home Improvement Contract Deposit Rules

Under Wis. Admin. Code ch. ATCP 110, home improvement contracts exceeding **$1,000** must be in writing and include:

| Requirement | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Written Contract** | Required for all home improvement contracts >$1,000 |
| **Start Date** | Must specify start date or circumstances determining start date |
| **Completion Date** | Must specify substantial completion date |
| **Total Price** | Must specify total price or method of calculation |
| **Cancellation Notice** | Must include consumer's right to cancel |

**Violations can result in:**
- Civil forfeitures
- Private rights of action
- Damages of **twice the amount of damages** plus attorney fees

#### 4.1.3 Escrow-Backed Advance Structures: Legal Analysis

##### Model 1: Tripartite Escrow (Homeowner-Contractor-GCSC)

In this structure, GCSC provides loan funding and an escrow agent (GCSC or third-party fiduciary) holds and disburses funds to contractors based on milestone completion.

**Regulatory Risks:**

| Risk Factor | Severity | Analysis |
|---|---|---|
| **Licensed lender requirement** | HIGH | If GCSC provides the loan and holds the escrow, DFI may view this as licensed lender activity requiring § 138.09 license if rates exceed 18% |
| **Consumer finance licensing** | HIGH | Consumer loans ≤$25K at >18% APR require DFI loan company license |
| **Money transmitter risk** | MEDIUM | If escrow involves transmission of funds across state lines or sovereign currency conversion, Ch. 217 licensing may apply |
| **Unlicensed public adjusting** | HIGH | If GCSC verifies insurance claim milestones (e.g., "insurer approved $X for shingles"), this could constitute claim negotiation under *In re Joshua Lesley* |
| **WCA applicability** | HIGH | Consumer-purpose loans trigger Wisconsin Consumer Act disclosures and right-to-cure |

##### Model 2: Independent Escrow Agent (Third-Party Fiduciary)

Using an independent escrow agent (e.g., Wisconsin-licensed attorney or financial institution) reduces GCSC's regulatory exposure:

| Factor | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Escrow Agent** | Wisconsin attorney or federally insured financial institution |
| **GCSC Role** | Lender only; does not hold construction funds |
| **Disbursement Trigger** | Contractor completion certificates; inspector verification |
| **Cost** | Typically $300–$800 per escrow plus wire fees |

**Legal Assessment:** Using an independent escrow agent reduces custody-related risk but does not eliminate licensed lender requirements if rates exceed 18% APR on consumer loans ≤$25K.

### 4.2 Money Transmitter Licensing (Wis. Stat. Chapter 217)

Wisconsin regulates money transmission through **Wis. Stat. Chapter 217**, the Money Transmitter law. The DFI has authority to license and supervise money transmitters operating in Wisconsin.

#### 4.2.1 License Requirements

| Requirement | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Statute** | Wis. Stat. Ch. 217 |
| **Regulator** | Wisconsin DFI — Division of Banking |
| **Bond** | $10,000 first location + $5,000 per additional location, capped at $300,000 |
| **Tangible Net Worth** | Requirements based on total assets |
| **Application** | Through NMLS |
| **Examination** | Periodic examinations by DFI |

#### 4.2.2 Activities Constituting "Money Transmission"

- Receiving money or monetary value for transmission to another location or person
- Selling or issuing payment instruments (checks, money orders, stored value)
- Receiving money for obligors for bill payment services
- **Facilitating repayment of loans** through collection and remittance of funds may constitute money transmission

#### 4.2.3 Post-January 1, 2025 Changes

All money transmitters engaging with Wisconsin persons must be licensed — **physical presence in Wisconsin is no longer required** for the licensing trigger. This means any out-of-state entity transmitting money to or from Wisconsin residents must obtain a Wisconsin money transmitter license.

## Section 5 — Insurance Claims, AOB & Assignment Framework

### 5.1 CRITICAL: New Wisconsin AOB Law (2025 Wis. Act 230 / § 632.11)

Wisconsin enacted the most significant AOB legislation in its history on **April 8, 2026**, with an effective date of **December 1, 2026**. This law fundamentally alters the landscape for residential contractors who seek to obtain assignment of insurance benefits.

#### 5.1.1 Scope of § 632.11

§ 632.11 applies to **post-loss assignments of rights or benefits under property insurance policies to residential contractors** for specific perils only:

| Covered Perils | Excluded Perils |
|---|---|
| Earthquake | Water leaks from frozen/broken pipes |
| Fire | Vehicle collisions |
| Flood | Normal wear and tear |
| Hailstorm | Vandalism (unless during covered event) |
| Tornado | Any peril not listed in left column |
| Windstorm | |
| Naturally occurring weather events with governor-declared state of emergency | |

**Key Limitation:** § 632.11 does NOT cover all property insurance claims. Assignments for water damage (frozen pipes), vehicle collisions, or routine maintenance are outside the statute's scope — but other restrictions (public adjuster rules, general contract law) still apply.

#### 5.1.2 Key Prohibitions on Contractors

| Subsection | Prohibition | Penalty |
|---|---|---|
| **§ 632.11(2)(a)** | **Prohibits contractors from waiving or reimbursing insurance deductibles** | Contract VOID; up to $5,000 forfeiture per violation |
| **§ 632.11(2)(b)** | **Prohibits contractors from representing an insured or negotiating directly with an insurer** | Contract VOID; up to $5,000 forfeiture per violation |

**Additional Requirements:**

| Requirement | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Written assignment contract** | Required |
| **Rights waiver notice** | Must include clear notice that insured is giving up rights |
| **Five-day cancellation period** | Insured may cancel within 5 business days |
| **Copy to insurer** | Contractor must provide copy of executed assignment to insurer within 5 business days |

#### 5.1.3 Effect on Insurance Payments

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Co-payee requirement** | Assignment merely requires insurer to name contractor as co-payee with named insured on checks |
| **Mortgagee rights preserved** | Assignment does not impair mortgagee's rights |
| **Insurer communication** | Insurer may still communicate with insured notwithstanding assignment |
| **Contractor remedies** | Contractor can only pursue insurer until legal rights exhausted; cannot threaten lien against insured for insurer non-payment |
| **Insurer subrogation** | Insurer gets automatic assignment of insured's rights against the contractor |
| **Bad faith claims** | Contractor obtains bad faith claim but **only for pre-assignment conduct** |

#### 5.1.4 Current Law (Until December 1, 2026)

Until § 632.11 takes effect, post-loss assignments remain valid under ***Pepsi-Cola Metro. Bottling Co. v. Emps. Ins. Co. of Wausau*** (2023 WI 42, 407 Wis. 2d 384, 990 N.W.2d 267):

- Anti-assignment clauses in insurance policies apply only to **pre-loss** assignments
- Post-loss assignments transfer the right to recovery after the insurer's liability has become fixed
- Contractors can obtain post-loss assignments and sue insurers for breach of contract
- This does **not** assign the entire policy — only the right to recover for a specific post-loss claim

### 5.2 Insurance Claim Payment Timing (Wis. Stat. § 628.46)

Wisconsin law imposes strict claim payment deadlines:

| Requirement | Detail | Statute |
|---|---|---|
| **Prompt payment** | Insurer must promptly pay every insurance claim | § 628.46(1) |
| **Overdue threshold** | Claim is overdue if not paid within **30 days** after insurer receives written notice of covered loss and amount of loss | § 628.46(1) |
| **Penalty interest** | **12% per year simple interest** on overdue payments | § 628.46(1) |
| **Non-responsibility defense** | Payment is not overdue if insurer has "reasonable proof" it is not responsible | § 628.46(2) |

**Key Case Law:**
- ***Kontowicz v. Am. Standard Ins. Co.*** (714 N.W.2d 105 (Wis. 2006)): § 628.46 unambiguously applies to all types of insurance coverage; prompt payment requirement is incorporated into insurance contract by operation of law
- ***Dilger v. Metro. Prop. Cas. Ins. Co.*** (868 N.W.2d 177 (Wis. Ct. App. 2015)): "Reasonable proof" of non-responsibility for interest on overdue claims equates to whether coverage issue was fairly debatable

### 5.3 Additional Living Expenses (ALE)

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Standard provision** | Most Wisconsin homeowners policies include ALE coverage |
| **Typical limit** | 10–30% of dwelling coverage depending on policy form |
| **Coverage scope** | Difference between normal living expenses and temporary increased costs |
| **ALE does NOT cover mortgage payments** | Only covers incremental costs above normal living expenses |
| **Time limit** | Typically covers the "shortest time required to repair or replace" |
| **Advance payments** | Insurance companies typically provide advance checks immediately after a disaster; deducted from final settlement |

## Section 7 — Public Adjuster, Representation & Advocacy Rules

### 7.1 Public Adjuster Licensing Framework

Wisconsin strictly regulates public adjusters under **Wis. Stat. Chapter 629**. Only licensed public adjusters may negotiate insurance claims on behalf of policyholders.

#### 7.1.1 Licensing Requirements

| Requirement | Detail | Statute |
|---|---|---|
| **Registration** | Required for non-resident public adjusters; residents may register but are not required to be licensed | § 629.03 |
| **Initial registration fee** | $50 (residents) | § 629.03 |
| **Surety bond** | **$20,000** | § 629.04 |
| **Written examination** | Required (residents) | § 629.03 |
| **Continuing education** | **15 hours biennially**, including 3 hours of ethics | § 629.05 |
| **Contract template filing** | Must file contract template with OCI | § 629.06 |

#### 7.1.2 Definition of "Public Adjuster"

> **Wis. Stat. § 629.01:** "Public adjuster" means any person who, **for compensation**, acts on behalf of an insured in **negotiating or settling a claim** for loss or damage under an insurance policy.

**Critical Principle — Status Determined by CONDUCT, Not Title:**

> Under ***In re Joshua Lesley*** (OCI Case No. 25-C46339, Wis. Div. Hearings & Appeals 2025): **"Public-adjuster" status is determined by CONDUCT, not title.** A person who performs public adjuster functions — regardless of whether they hold themselves out as a "public adjuster" — is subject to Chapter 629 regulation.

### 7.2 Prohibited Practices for Public Adjusters

| Prohibition | Statute | Penalty |
|---|---|---|
| Accepting assignment of policy proceeds | § 629.07 | Contract unenforceable; OCI discipline |
| Requiring insured to use particular contractor | § 629.06 | OCI discipline; license revocation |
| Having financial interest in subject of claim beyond compensation | § 629.06 | OCI discipline; license revocation |
| Representing insurer and insured simultaneously | § 629.06 | License revocation |
| Charging excessive fees | § 629.06 | Restitution; license discipline |

#### The § 629.07 AOB Prohibition

> **Wis. Stat. § 629.07:** "A public adjuster may not request or accept from an insured an assignment of the insured's rights or benefits under an insurance policy. An insurer is not required to honor an assignment made in violation of this section."

**This prohibition is absolute.** Public adjusters cannot accept AOB assignments under any circumstances. This eliminates a potential pathway for claim financing through licensed public adjuster channels.

### 7.3 The *In re Joshua Lesley* Case — CRITICAL

This 2025 OCI decision is the single most important case for GCSC in Wisconsin. It establishes the conduct-based test for public adjuster status and confirms that contractors who engage in claim activities risk being found unlicensed public adjusters.

#### 7.3.1 Facts of the Case

| Fact | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Respondent** | Dwelling contractor performing residential repair work |
| **Conduct** | Combined repair work with claim-handling functions |
| **AOB** | Used standard AOB transferring all insurance proceeds to himself |
| **Marketing** | Marketed "insurance claim support" to homeowners |
| **Estimates** | Submitted his own inflated repair estimate directly to insurer |
| **Negotiation** | Negotiated with insurer for higher claim payments |

#### 7.3.2 Holding

> **"Public-adjuster status is determined by CONDUCT, not title."**

A dwelling contractor who:
- Prepares, submits, or negotiates first-party claims for compensation
- Combines repair work with claim-handling functions reserved for licensed adjusters
- Uses AOB to transfer insurance proceeds to himself
- Markets "insurance claim support" services
- Submits repair estimates to insurers as claim advocacy

**...is illegally acting as an unlicensed public adjuster in violation of Chapter 629.**

#### 7.3.3 Implications for GCSC

| Activity | Status |
|---|---|
| GCSC having contractors negotiate claims with insurers on behalf of homeowners | **BLOCKED** — Unlicensed public adjusting |
| GCSC having contractors submit repair estimates directly to insurers as claim advocacy | **BLOCKED** — Unlicensed public adjusting |
| Any AOB arrangement must comply with § 632.11 requirements after December 1, 2026 | **REQUIRED** |
| The "repair first, negotiate later" model | **LEGALLY RISKY** in Wisconsin |
| GCSC providing platform features that facilitate contractor-claim negotiation | **BLOCKED** — Aiding and abetting unlicensed public adjusting |

### 7.4 Who May Negotiate with the Insurance Company

| Party | Authority to Negotiate | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| **Policyholder (homeowner)** | Full authority | Contractual right under policy |
| **Licensed public adjuster** | Full authority (representing policyholder) | § 629.01–.06 |
| **Attorney** | Full authority (representing policyholder) | Attorney-client relationship; exempt from adjuster licensing |
| **The insured/policyholder themselves** | Full authority | Own claim |
| **Contractor** | **NONE** (unless also licensed PA) | § 629; *In re Joshua Lesley* |
| **GCSC / SmartContractor** | **NONE** | No insurance license of any kind |

### 7.5 Contractors and Public Adjusting: The Bright-Line Rule

A contractor may **NOT**:
- Interpret insurance policy language for a homeowner
- Advise a homeowner about coverage limits or exclusions
- Advise a homeowner about duties under the policy (e.g., mitigation obligations)
- Submit a claim or supplemental claim on a homeowner's behalf
- Communicate with the insurer about scope of damages or pricing
- Attend adjuster meetings as the homeowner's "representative"
- Use software (e.g., Xactimate) to prepare "estimates" that function as claim demands
- Negotiate claim settlement amounts with insurers

A contractor **CAN**:
- Provide a repair estimate for work the contractor will perform
- Perform the contracted work
- Accept payment for work performed
- Communicate factual information (e.g., "We found hail damage on the roof")
- Be named as a co-payee on insurance checks under § 632.11 (after Dec. 1, 2026)

## Section 8 — Token Collateral, Digital Assets & Crypto Regulations

### 8.1 Wisconsin DFI Position on Virtual Currency

Wisconsin occupies a **favorable position** for pure virtual currency activities. The DFI has issued explicit guidance that it does **not** regulate virtual currency.

#### 8.1.1 DFI Virtual Currency Guidance

> **"Wis. Stat. ch. 217, the 'Money Transmitter' law, does not currently give the Department of Financial Institutions the authority to regulate virtual currency. The Division of Banking is therefore unable to license or supervise companies whose business activities are limited to those involving virtual currency."** — Wisconsin DFI

**However:**
> **"Should the transmission of virtual currency include the involvement of sovereign currency, it may be subject to licensure depending on how the transaction is structured."** — Wisconsin DFI

#### 8.1.2 Wisconsin Did NOT Adopt Virtual Currency Component of Model Money Transmission Modernization Act

- Wisconsin enacted the Money Transmitter Modernization Act through **2023 WI Act 267**
- However, Wisconsin **deliberately excluded** the optional virtual currency component
- This was an intentional legislative choice to maintain the status quo: **DFI does not regulate pure virtual currency**
- If virtual currency transmission involves sovereign currency (USD), licensure under Chapter 217 may still be required

### 8.2 Money Transmitter Requirements (Post-January 1, 2025)

| Requirement | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Physical presence eliminated** | All money transmitters engaging with Wisconsin persons must be licensed, regardless of physical presence |
| **Bond** | $10,000 first location + $5,000 per additional location, capped at $300,000 |
| **Tangible net worth** | Requirements based on total assets |
| **NMLS** | Required for application |

### 8.3 Token Collateral for GCSC Loans

Wisconsin does not have a specific statute addressing the use of digital assets as collateral for loans. The regulatory analysis proceeds by analogy:

#### 8.3.1 Custodial Token Collateral (GCSC Holds Private Keys)

| Factor | Analysis | Risk Level |
|---|---|---|
| Money transmitter licensing | If custodial holding involves only virtual currency (no fiat conversion), DFI guidance suggests **no license required** | LOW-MEDIUM |
| Licensed lender requirement | If loan is ≤$25K to individual consumer at >18%, § 138.09 license required | HIGH |
| Securities law | If collateral token is a security, securities licensing may apply | MEDIUM |
| Liquidation mechanism | Forced liquidation of collateral may be challenged under WCA if consumer loan | MEDIUM |
| UCC Article 9 | Token collateral may be perfected via UCC filing; Wisconsin has adopted UCC Article 12 (controllable electronic records) | LIKELY AVAILABLE |

**Conclusion:** Custodial token collateral in Wisconsin is **DEMO_ONLY** pending legal review of licensed lender requirements and consumer-purpose loan analysis.

#### 8.3.2 Non-Custodial Token Collateral (User Controls Keys)

| Factor | Analysis | Risk Level |
|---|---|---|
| Money transmitter licensing | User controls keys; GCSC has no unilateral execution ability → likely NOT money transmitter | LOW |
| Licensed lender requirement | Still required if loan is ≤$25K to individual at >18% | HIGH |
| Smart contract risk | Smart contract bugs or exploits create liability; Wisconsin has no smart contract immunity statute | MEDIUM |
| Liquidation trigger | Automated liquidation via oracle may avoid "unilateral" element but untested in Wisconsin courts | UNCERTAIN |

**Conclusion:** Non-custodial token collateral may be viable in Wisconsin **IF** GCSC obtains a § 138.09 license (or structures loans outside licensed lender thresholds) AND the smart contract architecture is reviewed by Wisconsin counsel. This remains **DEMO_ONLY** pending legal review.

## Section 9 — Mortgage, Loss Draft & Property Law

### 9.1 Standard Loss Draft Process in Wisconsin

When a mortgage exists on damaged property, the insurance claim check for dwelling damage is typically made payable to **both the insured and the mortgage holder**:

| Stage | Typical Timeline | Detail |
|---|---|---|
| Insurer issues joint check | Day 0 | Payable to homeowner + mortgagee |
| Homeowner sends check to servicer | Day 1–5 | Delays common |
| Servicer endorses and deposits | Day 5–10 | "Promptly" interpreted loosely |
| Initial disbursement (30–50%) | Day 10–20 | After contractor selected and estimate approved |
| Mid-project inspection | Variable | Inspector verifies progress |
| Final disbursement | Day 30–60+ | After final inspection and certificate of completion |

**Total timeline from claim payment to full fund release: 30–60+ days.**

### 9.2 Mortgagee Rights Under § 632.11

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **AOB does not impair mortgagee** | An assignment of benefits to a contractor does not impair a mortgagee's right under the policy or mortgage documents |
| **Mortgage holder retention** | Mortgage holders retain their full rights after AOB execution |
| **Co-payee requirement** | Insurers continue to name mortgagees as co-payees even after AOB execution |
| **Loss draft process unchanged** | The standard loss draft/mortgagee endorsement process continues to apply |

### 9.3 Assignment of Claim Proceeds

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Pre-December 1, 2026** | Post-loss assignments generally valid under *Pepsi-Cola* (2023) |
| **Post-December 1, 2026** | Assignments to residential contractors limited by § 632.11 — written contract required, 5-day cancellation right, deductible waiver prohibited, contractor negotiation prohibited |
| **Mortgage holders** | NOT bound by AOB agreements |
| **Loss draft checks** | Must still include mortgagee as payee |

### 9.4 Wisconsin Mechanic's Liens

| Element | Detail | Statute |
|---|---|---|
| **Lien right** | Contractors who improve real property have lien rights | Wis. Stat. § 779.01 et seq. |
| **Notice of lien** | Must file within 6 months of last work | § 779.06 |
| **Lien foreclosure** | Action must be commenced within 2 years of filing | § 779.06 |
| **Priority** | Mechanic's lien generally dates to first work, giving priority over subsequent liens | § 779.02 |

**GCSC Implication:** If GCSC provides a loan to a contractor who performs work, the contractor's mechanic's lien rights may provide indirect security. However, a GCSC loan itself does not independently create mechanic's lien rights.

### 9.5 Wisconsin Foreclosure and Homestead Law

| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Judicial foreclosure** | Wisconsin requires judicial foreclosure; non-judicial foreclosure is NOT permitted for residential mortgages |
| **Timeline** | 6–12 months typical; longer if contested |
| **Deficiency judgment** | Available for recourse mortgages |
| **Homestead exemption** | Wisconsin provides a homestead exemption of up to $75,000 (single) / $150,000 (married) that protects primary residence equity from creditor claims | Wis. Stat. § 815.20 |

**GCSC Implication:** Wisconsin's homestead exemption limits GCSC's recovery on unsecured loans to homeowners. A judgment against a Wisconsin homeowner may be difficult to collect if the homeowner's primary residence equity is within the exemption amount.
