## 1. State Summary

Massachusetts is a **highly regulated environment** for SmartContractor products. The state imposes significant restrictions on contractor involvement in financing, a newly expanded money-transmission regime covering virtual currencies (effective Jan 1, 2026), strong consumer-protection laws with mandatory treble damages, and common-law-based post-loss assignment of benefits.

### Key Facts at a Glance

| Factor | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| Usury cap | **20%** for loans under **$6,000** (Mass. Gen. Laws Ch. 140, § 96); above 12% APR triggers small-loan licensing |
| Small-loan threshold | $6,000 or less |
| Small-loan licensing | 209 CMR; Division of Banks |
| Contractor financing | **PROHIBITED** — contractors cannot offer, arrange, or negotiate financing secured by a mortgage on the home |
| Money transmission | Mass. Gen. Laws Ch. 169B (effective Jan 1, 2026); virtual currency treated as "monetary value" |
| AOB status | Generally permitted **post-loss** under common law (*Wilson v. Hill*, 3 Met. 66 (1841)) |
| Public adjuster | License **required**; MA was first state to license public adjusters (1880s) |
| Consumer protection | **Treble damages** + attorney's fees under Ch. 93A for knowing/willful violations |
| Escrow regulation | Division of Banks regulates escrow activities |

### Top-Level Blockers

1. **Contractor financing prohibition** — M.G.L. c. 142A, § 17 blocks any contractor from acting as mortgage broker, arranging loans, or offering financing secured by the home. This is a **per se** violation of Ch. 93A (Consumer Protection Act).
2. **Virtual currency money transmission** — M.G.L. c. 169B (effective Jan 1, 2026) characterizes most transactional virtual currencies (Bitcoin, USDC) as "monetary value." Token collateral lock/liquidation likely triggers licensure.
3. **Small-loan licensing** — Loans of $6,000 or less at rates above 12% APR require a small-loan agency license from the Division of Banks.
4. **High consumer-protection exposure** — Ch. 93A provides mandatory treble damages and attorney's fees; class-action exposure is significant.

**All live product flows should be marked BLOCKED pending review by Massachusetts-licensed counsel.**

## 3. Lending / Finance Licensing

### Small Loan Agency License (Mass. Gen. Laws Ch. 140, § 96; 209 CMR 20.00)
- **License required** for any person making loans of **$6,000 or less** and charging interest **in excess of 12% per annum**.
- **20% hard usury cap** applies to all loans under $6,000 — no licensee may contract for a rate exceeding 20% APR.
- **Net worth**: Minimum $75,000 adjusted net worth (209 CMR 20.03(2)(a)1).
- **Financial statements**: Audited or reviewed statements required annually.
- **Fees**: $1,000 license fee + $300 investigation fee + surcharges.
- **Exemptions**: Banks, credit unions, and certain federally regulated entities.

### Sales Finance Company License (Mass. Gen. Laws Ch. 255B, Ch. 255D; 209 CMR 20.00)
- Required for retail installment sales financing.
- Minimum net worth of $20,000 (may be waived on renewal).
- Surety bond up to $10,000 may be required.

### Mortgage Lender/Broker License (Mass. Gen. Laws Ch. 255E; 209 CMR 42.00)
- Required for any mortgage lending or brokering activities.
- NMLS licensing required.
- Recent amendments (effective Oct 2025) require disclosure of NMLS unique identifier.

### Truth in Lending (Mass. Gen. Laws Ch. 140D; 209 CMR 32.00)
- Massachusetts has its own Truth in Lending law in addition to federal TILA.
- Specific variable-rate adjustment notice requirements apply.

### Loan Brokering
- Entities brokering loans for home-improvement contractors may trigger small-loan agency licensing requirements (Selected Opinion 98-190).

**SmartContractor Implication**: Any loan product offered to homeowners or contractors in MA that is $6,000 or less and charges over 12% APR requires a small-loan agency license. Commercial-purpose loans may be exempt but require careful structuring.

## 4. Escrow-Backed Contractor Advance Rules

### Division of Banks Escrow Authority
The **Massachusetts Division of Banks** regulates escrow activities in the Commonwealth. Any escrow arrangement holding funds for home-improvement contracts or insurance-claim proceeds falls under DOB oversight.

### Escrow Requirements for Contractor Advances
- **Written escrow agreement mandatory** — All funds held in escrow for contractor advances must be governed by a written agreement specifying disbursement conditions, timing, and beneficiary rights.
- **Segregated accounts** — Escrow funds must be maintained in a segregated, non-commingled account at a Massachusetts-licensed financial institution or a federally insured bank.
- **Interest-bearing** — Escrow accounts should be interest-bearing where applicable, with interest disposition disclosed to the homeowner.

### Prohibited Escrow Arrangements
- **No contractor self-escrow** — A home-improvement contractor may not serve as the sole escrow agent for funds related to its own contract. An independent licensed escrow agent or financial institution must hold the funds.
- **No commingling with operating funds** — Escrowed advance funds cannot be commingled with the contractor's general operating accounts.
- **Advance deposit cap** — Under Ch. 142A, § 2, no contractor may demand more than **one-third of the total contract price** in advance (except for special-order materials). Any escrow-backed advance structure must respect this cap.

### Escrow Disbursement Triggers
- Funds may only be disbursed upon:
  1. Completion of defined milestones verified by an independent inspector;
  2. Submission of lien waivers from subcontractors and material suppliers;
  3. Homeowner written approval of completed work; and/or
  4. Court order or mutual written agreement of all parties.

### Escrow-Backed Advance as "Safe Harbor"
An escrow-backed advance — where funds are held by an independent escrow agent and disbursed only upon verified completion — may provide a pathway around the Ch. 142A, § 17 contractor-financing prohibition **only if**:
- The contractor plays **no role** in arranging, offering, or negotiating the advance;
- The lender is **structurally independent** of the contractor (no compensation, referral fees, or marketing agreements);
- The homeowner **directly contracts** with the lender; and
- The escrow agent is a **licensed bank, credit union, or licensed escrow provider**.

**SmartContractor Implication**: Escrow-backed advances are theoretically viable but carry significant structural risk. The contractor must be entirely walled off from the financing arrangement. Any appearance of contractor involvement in arranging or facilitating the advance creates a Ch. 142A, § 17 violation and a per se Ch. 93A breach. All escrow structures require Massachusetts counsel review and DOB compliance confirmation.

## 5. Token Collateral / Crypto Regulation

### Massachusetts Money Transmission Law (Mass. Gen. Laws Ch. 169B)
- **Effective date**: January 1, 2026 (licensing); applications accepted from July 1, 2025.
- **Coverage**: Requires licensure for selling/issuing payment instruments, selling/issuing stored value, or receiving money for transmission from a person in Massachusetts.
- **Business exemption**: Only applies to transactions for "personal, family or household purposes."
- **Virtual currency**: DOB FAQ #19 explicitly states — **"Generally, yes, because the Division considers most common transactional virtual currencies (for example, Bitcoin or USDC) to be a form of 'monetary value', as defined in MGL c. 169B, §1."**
- **Closed-loop exception**: Virtual currencies that are not convertible and cannot be used as a medium for exchange (e.g., video game currency) are NOT considered monetary value.

### Token Collateral for Lending
- **No specific statute** addresses the use of cryptocurrency as collateral for a loan.
- A token collateral lock/escrow/liquidation mechanism that involves **receiving, holding, transmitting, or liquidating virtual currency on behalf of Massachusetts residents** would likely be characterized as money transmission requiring licensure.
- **Surety bond**: New money transmitters must maintain a bond of the greater of $100,000 or 100% of average daily money transmission liability, capped at $500,000.
- **Permissible investments**: Licensees must maintain highly liquid investments backing 100% of outstanding transmission obligations.

## 6. Insurance Claim Advances

### Emergency Advance Payments
- The Massachusetts DOI confirms: **"The first payment will likely be an emergency advance and may include additional living expenses if the damage to your home renders it uninhabitable."**
- An insurance adjuster makes a preliminary assessment based on **actual cash value (ACV)**, and the insured should receive a check for that amount soon after.
- For **replacement cost** policies, the insurer pays the difference between replacement cost and ACV once proof of repairs is received.

### Claim Processing Timeframes
- Insurance companies are expected to respond to claims and settle for a "fair and reasonable amount as soon as possible."
- Large losses (fire claims) typically take "several weeks to several months" to settle in full.

### Additional Living Expenses (ALE)
- Standard homeowners policies include ALE/loss of use coverage, typically 20% of dwelling coverage limit.
- Covers hotel bills, reasonable restaurant meals, and other living costs above normal expenses.
- Insured must keep all receipts.

### No Statutory Restrictions on Emergency Advances Found
- No Massachusetts statute restricts insurers from making emergency advance payments to homeowners or contractors.
- However, insurers are not required to make advances; it is at their discretion.

**SmartContractor Implication**: An insurance claim advance product that provides funds to homeowners pending insurance company payment is not directly prohibited, but may trigger lending licensing if structured as a loan with repayment. Assignment of claim proceeds as security would need to comply with AOB rules (see Section 7).

## 7. Assignment of Benefits (AOB)

### Massachusetts Law on Post-Loss Assignment
Massachusetts follows the **majority rule** under common law, as established in ***Wilson v. Hill*, 3 Met. 66 (Mass. 1841)**:

> "These considerations... do not apply to a case where the assured, after a loss, assigns his right to recover that loss; it would stand on the same footing as the assignment of a debt, or right to recover a sum of money actually due, which like the assignment of any other chose in action, would give the assignee an equitable interest, and a right to recover in the name of the assignor, subject to set-off and all other equities."

### Key Principles
1. **Post-loss assignments are generally valid** even if the insurance policy contains an anti-assignment clause. The assignment is of the claim (a chose in action), not of the policy itself.
2. **Pre-loss assignments** of the policy are prohibited without insurer consent.
3. **No comprehensive AOB statute**: Unlike Florida (§ 627.7152), Massachusetts does not have a specific statute comprehensively governing assignment of benefits agreements for property insurance claims.

### Requirements for Valid Assignment
- Must be a **written agreement** executed by the assignor.
- Should clearly define the scope of assigned rights (emergency services only, or full claim).
- Should include itemized cost estimates.
- Should provide for rescission/cancellation rights.

**SmartContractor Implication**: Post-loss assignment of insurance benefits appears legally permissible in Massachusetts under common law. However, the absence of a comprehensive AOB statute means significant uncertainty around required notice provisions, cancellation windows, font-size/disclosure requirements, and contractor collection restrictions. Massachusetts counsel should draft AOB agreements specifically for MA common law.

## 8. Public Adjuster / Insurance Representation

### Licensing Requirements (Mass. Gen. Laws Ch. 175, § 172; 211 CMR 50.00)
Massachusetts was the **first state in the nation** to license public insurance adjusters (1880s). Key requirements:

| Requirement | Detail |
|-------------|--------|
| Age | At least 21 years old |
| Experience | 2 years performing services in connection with adjusting property losses |
| Examination | Must pass licensing examination |
| Written contract | Must use a contract approved by the Commissioner of Insurance |
| Background check | CORI background check required |
| License fee | $200 (non-refundable) |
| Continuing education | 15 hours CE per renewal year |

### Scope of Representation
Public adjusters may represent insureds in:
- Assessment of damages
- **Negotiation**
- **Settlement**
- Appraisal
- Reference (arbitration)

### Contract Requirements
- Must be in writing on DOI-approved form.
- Must be signed by Named Insured or authorized agent.
- Must include **3-day rescission right** (insured can cancel by certified letter within 3 days).
- After 3-day period, insured can still cancel but public adjuster has lien for agreed fee on proceeds.

### Fee Limit
- No statutory cap on fees.
- DOI model contracts reference fee "not in excess of 10% of the claim payment."
- Fees are negotiable and typically contingency-based.

### CRITICAL: Only Licensed Public Adjusters May Negotiate Claims
Under Massachusetts law, **only licensed public insurance adjusters** may negotiate and settle insurance claims on behalf of homeowners. Contractors, restoration companies, or SmartContractor representatives **must NOT** engage in claim negotiation, settlement advocacy, or other activities that could be construed as public adjusting without a license.

**SmartContractor Implication**: SmartContractor and its contractor partners must strictly avoid any activity that could be characterized as public adjusting, including:
- Negotiating claim amounts with insurers
- Preparing claim estimates for submission to insurers on behalf of homeowners
- Advocating for claim coverage decisions
- Settling claims with insurance adjusters

Any such activity should be referred to a licensed public insurance adjuster or attorney.

## 9. Mortgage / Loss Draft

### Standard Industry Practice
- The Massachusetts DOI confirms: "If you have a mortgage, your lender has an interest in making sure the home is rebuilt or that your loan is paid in full. Your mortgage lender requires you to add them as an additional insured on your homeowners policy. Because of this, the insurer is obligated to include them on the check for major repairs. You'll need to work with your mortgage lender to get their signature on any payments."

### Loss Draft / Claim Proceeds Rules
- For major repairs, insurance claim checks will include the **mortgagee as a co-payee**.
- The mortgage lender must endorse the check.
- Lenders typically disburse funds in draws based on repair progress inspections.
- If the loan is current, initial disbursement may be up to $40,000 or 33% of proceeds (Fannie Mae guidelines commonly followed).
- If the loan is delinquent, stricter disbursement schedules apply.

**SmartContractor Implication**: Any product that routes repayment from insurance claim proceeds must account for mortgagee/loss draft procedures. The mortgage lender's interest takes priority, and claim checks will require mortgagee endorsement, complicating direct repayment routing to SmartContractor or contractors.
