# Ohio (OH) — State Compliance File

> **Moderate Regulation** | **Status: BLOCKED pending legal review**
>
> Ohio presents a moderately complex regulatory environment for GCSC products. The state has no comprehensive AOB statute (common law governs), multiple overlapping consumer lending license regimes, an 8% general usury cap with criminal penalties above 25%, blockchain recognition via SB 220 (2018), and strict public adjuster prohibitions. The Ohio Department of Insurance and Ohio Department of Commerce — Division of Financial Institutions (DFI) are the primary financial regulators.

## 1. State Overview

**State Population:** ~11.8 million

**Regulatory Characteristics:**

| Characteristic | Detail |
|---------------|--------|
| Regulatory Complexity | **Moderate** |
| Primary Insurance Regulator | Ohio Department of Insurance (insurance.ohio.gov) |
| Primary Financial Regulator | Ohio DFI — Division of Financial Institutions (com.ohio.gov) |
| Contractor Licensing | OCILB (5 specialty trades only); general contractors regulated locally |
| AOB Statute | **No comprehensive statute** — common law governs |
| Blockchain Recognition | **Yes** — SB 220 (2018) amends UETA to recognize blockchain records/signatures |
| General Usury Cap | **8%** on loans under $100,000 unless licensed (ORC 1343.01) |
| Criminal Usury | >25% APR is a felony unless otherwise authorized |
| Money Transmitter Law | ORC Chapter 1315.01 et seq. |

**Key Characteristics for GCSC:**

- **No comprehensive Assignment of Benefits (AOB) statute** exists. AOB enforceability is governed by common law and contract principles. The Ohio Supreme Court held in *Pilkington N. Am., Inc. v. Travelers Cas. & Sur. Co.* (2006) that post-loss assignments of insurance claims are generally valid even in the face of anti-assignment clauses, but a 2021 federal Sixth Circuit decision (*Blue Ash Auto Body, Inc. v. State Farm*) created uncertainty by upholding anti-assignment language in auto policies. AOBs in Ohio are **not prohibited** but their enforceability depends on specific policy language and whether the loss has occurred.

- **Multiple lending license types** are required depending on loan size and structure: Small Loan Act (loans up to $5,000), Short-Term Loan Act (loans up to $1,000), Consumer Installment Loan Act (CILA, minimum 6-month terms, up to 25% APR), and General Loan Law for non-mortgage consumer loans. **Commercial/business-purpose loans generally do not require a consumer lending license**, but all consumer-purpose loans require licensure.

- **Ohio recognizes blockchain records and smart contracts** under SB 220 (2018), which amended the Uniform Electronic Transactions Act to include blockchain-secured records as valid electronic records and signatures. However, **no specific virtual currency/cryptocurrency regulatory framework** exists, and the Money Transmitter Act does not explicitly address digital assets.

- **Public insurance adjusters must be licensed** by the Ohio Department of Insurance with a Certificate of Authority, $1,000 surety bond, and examination. Contractors **cannot** act as public adjusters or negotiate claim settlements on behalf of homeowners.

- **Contractor licensing** is limited at the state level to five specialty trades (electrical, HVAC, plumbing, hydronics, refrigeration) through OCILB. General contractors are regulated at the municipal level. Home construction service suppliers (residential contracts over $25,000) must register under ORC Chapter 4722.

- **All GCSC live operations are BLOCKED pending legal review** due to uncertainties around AOB enforceability, consumer lending licensing requirements for any homeowner-facing products, token collateral treatment, and insurance claim advance structures.

## 3. Lending & Finance Licensing

### 3.1 Consumer Lending License Requirements

Ohio requires specific licenses for consumer-purpose lending depending on loan structure:

#### Small Loan Act (ORC Sections 1321.01–1321.19)
- **Applies to**: Loans of $5,000 or less for personal, family, or household purposes
- **License required**: Yes — from Ohio Superintendent of Financial Institutions
- **Rate cap**: 8% general usury limit per ORC 1343.01, but higher rates permitted under specific exceptions
- **Key restriction**: Section 1321.141 prohibits loans of $1,000 or less, or loans with duration of 1 year or less
- **Minimum net worth**: $25,000 cash or readily accessible funds
- **License fee**: $300 annually per location
- **Investigation fee**: $200 (non-refundable)
- **Exemptions**: Banks, savings banks, trust companies, savings and loan associations, credit unions, and their regulated subsidiaries

#### Short-Term Loan Act (ORC Sections 1321.35–1321.48)
- **Applies to**: Short-term consumer loans
- **Loan size limit**: $1,000 maximum
- **Term**: Minimum 91 days, maximum 1 year; can be shorter if monthly payment < 6% gross monthly income or 7% net monthly income
- **Rate cap**: 28% APR maximum (ORC 1321.40)
- **License fee**: $1,000 per location ($500 if issued on or after July 1)
- **Net worth**: $100,000 minimum ($50,000 for nonprofit corporations)
- **Surety bond**: $100,000 ($50,000 for nonprofit corporations)
- **Prohibited**: Making loans by phone or mail (ORC 1321.36(B))

#### Consumer Installment Loan Act / CILA (ORC Sections 1321.62–1321.702)
- **Applies to**: Consumer installment loans
- **Minimum term**: 6 months
- **Rate cap**: 25% annually (ORC 1321.68)
- **License required**: Yes, unless exclusion applies
- **License fee**: $300 annual registration + $200 investigation fee
- **Net worth**: $50,000 minimum
- **Assets**: $50,000 per license
- **Prohibited**: Refinancing within first 120 days of loan term (ORC 1321.631)
- **Recordkeeping**: Extensive electronic spreadsheet requirements per ORC 1321.66
- **No person selling tangible goods/services may hold CILA license for that business** (ORC 1321.644)

#### General Loan Law (ORC Sections 1321.51–1321.60)
- **Certificate of Registration required** for non-mortgage consumer loans
- **License fee**: $300 annually
- **Covers**: Consumer loans not covered by other specific acts

#### Residential Mortgage Lending Act (RMLA)
- **Consolidated** mortgage lending regulation under HB 199
- **Applies to**: Residential mortgage loans
- **Licensing**: Required through Ohio DFI

### 3.2 Commercial/Business-Purpose Lending

- **Ohio generally does NOT require a lending license for bona fide commercial/business-purpose loans** that are not primarily for personal, family, or household purposes
- The Small Loan Act, Short-Term Loan Act, and CILA all regulate **consumer-purpose** loans
- **Business loans are exempt from the 8% usury cap** under ORC 1343.01
- **Loans exceeding $100,000** are exempt from the general usury limit
- **Loans secured by mortgage or deed of trust** are exempt from the general usury limit

### 3.3 Interest Rate / Usury Summary

| Rate Type | Threshold | Citation |
|-----------|-----------|----------|
| General civil usury | **8% per year** | ORC 1343.01 |
| Criminal usury | >25% per annum (felony unless otherwise authorized) | ORC 1343.01 |
| Ohio-chartered bank max | Up to 25% plus fees | ORC 1343.01 |
| Short-term loan cap | 28% APR | ORC 1321.40 |
| CILA cap | 25% APR | ORC 1321.68 |
| Federal preemption | National banks may export home state rates | Federal law |

### 3.4 Loan Brokering

- Consumer loan brokering / brokering of residential mortgage loans requires appropriate licensure
- Commercial loan brokering generally does not require a specific license in Ohio

## 4. Escrow & Money Transmitter Regulation

### 4.1 Money Transmitter Act (ORC Chapter 1315.01 et seq.)

Ohio's Money Transmitter Act regulates persons engaged in the business of money transmission:

- **License required** for: "directly or indirectly receiving money or its equivalent and delivering it by any means, method, or service to the same or another person at the same or another time or place" (ORC 1315.01)
- **Minimum net worth**: $500,000
- **License application**: Filed through NMLS
- **Surety bond**: Required; amount varies based on transmission volume
- **Exemptions**: Banks, credit unions, and certain regulated financial institutions

**Key Provisions:**

| Provision | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| Licensing | Required through Ohio DFI via NMLS |
| Net worth | $500,000 minimum |
| Bond | Based on prior year transmission volume |
| Permissible investments | Required to maintain assets ≥ outstanding transmission obligations |
| Audit | Annual audited financial statements required |
| Records | 3-year retention required |

### 4.2 Digital Asset / Virtual Currency Treatment

- **Ohio's Money Transmitter Act does NOT explicitly include "virtual currencies" or "monetary value"** in its definitions
- The Ohio Department of Commerce has **not published formal guidance** on virtual currency regulation under the MTA
- However, the Division of Financial Institutions maintains a **list of licensed cryptocurrency kiosk/ATM companies**, suggesting some level of regulatory awareness and oversight
- **Whether digital asset/token collateral management requires a money transmitter license is UNCLEAR**
- **No specific virtual currency/cryptocurrency regulatory framework** has been enacted

### 4.3 Escrow Regulation

- The **Ohio Department of Commerce — Division of Industrial Compliance** has authority to regulate escrow activity in the state
- Escrow agents handling real estate transactions must comply with applicable Ohio Department of Commerce regulations
- **Ohio has no statute specifically governing loss draft handling** for insurance proceeds; standard mortgage contract provisions govern
- Mortgage servicers typically hold loss draft proceeds in escrow and disburse incrementally based on repair progress inspections
- For current loans: Initial disbursement up to greater of $40,000 or 33% of proceeds (per Fannie Mae guidelines)
- For delinquent loans: More restrictive disbursement schedules apply

### 4.4 Implications for GCSC

| Activity | Regulatory Risk | Notes |
|----------|----------------|-------|
| Repayment routing from insurance proceeds | **MEDIUM** | May implicate money transmitter licensing if GCSC receives and forwards funds |
| Token collateral custody/management | **HIGH** | Unclear whether holding token collateral constitutes money transmission |
| Escrow of claim proceeds | **MEDIUM** | Ohio Department of Commerce may regulate escrow activity |
| Third-party payment processing | **LOW-MEDIUM** | Use of licensed payment processors reduces MT risk |

**Money Transmitter Assessment**: Any GCSC product that involves receiving funds from homeowners, insurers, or contractors and forwarding those funds to another party should be reviewed for potential money transmitter licensing requirements. Token collateral custody and liquidation mechanisms present particular uncertainty.

## 5. Contractor Licensing & Registration

### 5.1 State-Level: OCILB (5 Specialty Trades Only)

The Ohio Construction Industry Licensing Board licenses only five commercial specialty trades:

| Trade | OCILB License Required |
|-------|----------------------|
| Electrical | Yes |
| HVAC | Yes |
| Plumbing | Yes |
| Hydronics | Yes |
| Refrigeration | Yes |
| **General Contracting** | **No — regulated locally** |

**OCILB Requirements:**
- 5 years experience as tradesperson, OR registered Ohio PE with 3 years construction experience
- Pass PSI examination (business/law + trade-specific)
- Minimum $500,000 contractor liability insurance
- Annual license: $60 renewal
- 8 hours continuing education annually (half in code for electrical)
- Assigned to ONE contracting company only (no DBAs)
- **OCILB does NOT license general contractors**

### 5.2 State-Level: Home Construction Service Supplier (HCSSA)

Under ORC Chapter 4722, contractors performing **residential** construction, remodeling, or renovation on contracts **exceeding $25,000** must register as a Home Construction Service Supplier with the Ohio Attorney General's Office.

**Key requirements:**
- Registration (not a license — no exam required)
- Minimum $250,000 general liability insurance
- Written contract required for all work
- **Down payment limit**: 10% of contract price (ORC 4722.04)
- Exception: May take 75% down payment on special order items that cannot be returned
- Excess cost notification required if costs exceed estimate by $5,000
- "Cost-plus contracts" exempt from many requirements

### 5.3 Local/Municipal Level

- General contractors, roofers, siding, and other non-OCILB trades are regulated at the **city/county level**
- Major cities (Columbus, Cleveland, Cincinnati) have their own registration/licensing requirements
- Typically require: general liability insurance, workers comp, bonding (amounts vary by jurisdiction)

### 5.4 Contractor Financing Implications for GCSC

- **Equipment financing** to licensed contractors for business purposes likely falls outside consumer lending regulation
- **Working capital loans** to properly licensed contractors for commercial purposes generally do not require consumer lending license
- Contractor must maintain appropriate license for their trade and jurisdiction
- **Important**: If financing is for residential homeowner projects, consumer lending laws may apply depending on loan structure

### 5.5 Consumer Protection for Residential Construction

- The Home Construction Service Supplier Act (ORC 4722) provides significant homeowner protections
- Prohibited acts include: commencing work without written contract, charging for unapproved excess costs, requiring excessive down payments
- Remedies: Owner may rescind contract or recover actual damages plus up to $5,000 in non-economic damages plus attorney fees
- All contracts must include specific disclosures

## 6. Blockchain, Token Collateral & Smart Contracts

### 6.1 Ohio Blockchain Legislation (SB 220 — 2018)

- Signed into law August 3, 2018
- Amended Ohio's Uniform Electronic Transactions Act (ORC Chapter 1306)
- Two key changes:
  1. **Electronic record**: "A record or contract that is secured through blockchain technology is considered to be in an electronic form and to be an electronic record"

## 7. Insurance Claims & Timelines

### 7.1 Ohio Insurance Claim Handling Timelines (Ohio Admin. Code 3901-1-54)

| Action | Timeline | Citation |
|--------|----------|----------|
| Acknowledge claim | 15 days of receiving notice | OAC 3901-1-54(F)(1) |
| Begin investigation | 21 days of receiving notice | OAC 3901-1-54(F)(2) |
| Accept or deny claim | 21 days of receiving proof of loss | OAC 3901-1-54(G)(1) |
| Pay accepted claim | 10 days after acceptance (if amount determined) | OAC 3901-1-54(G)(3) |
| Status updates (if extended) | Every 45 days | OAC 3901-1-54(G)(1) |

### 7.2 Key Rules

- **Payment timing**: Insurer must tender payment within **10 days after acceptance** if the amount is determined and not in dispute
- **Prompt payment**: Delays beyond these timelines may constitute unfair claims settlement practices
- **Private right of action**: The Unfair Claims Settlement Practices Act **does NOT create a private right of action** for policyholders, but violations may be admissible as evidence of bad faith (*Strack v. Westfield Cos.*, 33 Ohio App.3d 336)
- **Bad faith claims**: Policyholder can sue for bad faith refusal to pay; must show (1) insurer refused to pay, and (2) refusal was not predicated on reasonable justification

### 7.3 Additional Living Expenses (ALE)

- Ohio does **not have a specific statute mandating ALE coverage or emergency advance payments**
- ALE coverage depends on policy terms
- However, insurers must disclose "all pertinent benefits, coverages or other provisions" (OAC 3901-1-54(E)(1))
- Emergency advance payments: No specific Ohio statute requires insurers to make emergency advance payments, but failure to make prompt payments may constitute an unfair practice

### 7.4 Claim Proceeds and Mortgagee

- Standard mortgage clauses require insurer to name mortgagee as additional loss payee
- Mortgage companies typically hold loss draft proceeds and disburse incrementally based on repair progress
- Fannie Mae guidelines: For current loans, servicer may release initial disbursement up to greater of $40,000 or 33% of proceeds
- **GCSC cannot interfere with mortgagee's rights to claim proceeds**

## 8. Assignment of Benefits (AOB)

### 8.1 AOB Status in Ohio: **ALLOWED BUT UNCERTAIN — NO COMPREHENSIVE STATUTE**

#### Key Legal Framework

**1. Ohio Supreme Court: Post-Loss Assignments Generally Valid**

In *Pilkington N. Am., Inc. v. Travelers Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 112 Ohio St.3d 482 (2006), the Ohio Supreme Court held:
- Insurance contracts receive "unique treatment post-loss"
- Assignment of an interest is valid **after** the occurrence of the loss, even in the face of an anti-assignment provision
- Post-loss assignment is treated as a transfer of a "chose in action" (the right to sue for the claim)
- Anti-assignment clauses in policies **do not prevent** post-loss assignments

**2. Federal Sixth Circuit: Anti-Assignment Clauses May Be Enforceable**

In *Blue Ash Auto Body, Inc. v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co.*, No. 21-3365, 2021 WL 5755632 (6th Cir. Dec. 3, 2021):
- The court upheld anti-assignment language in auto insurance policies
- Distinguishable facts from Pilkington (involved body shop assignments, not successor-in-interest)
- Court enforced policy language requiring insurer consent for assignment
- **This decision created uncertainty about AOB enforceability in Ohio**

**3. Ohio Has NO Comprehensive AOB Statute**

Unlike Florida, Louisiana, and other states, Ohio has **not enacted** a comprehensive AOB reform statute. Key implications:
- No required AOB notice/cancellation window
- No mandatory font-size or signature requirements
- No prohibition on AOBs
- No limitation on contractor use of AOBs
- Common law contract principles govern

**4. Practical Implications for GCSC**

| Factor | Ohio Treatment |
|--------|---------------|
| AOB validity (post-loss) | Generally valid per *Pilkington* |
| Anti-assignment clauses | May be enforceable per *Blue Ash Auto Body* |
| Required notices | None specified by statute |
| Cancellation window | Not specified by statute |
| Contractor limitations | None specific to AOBs |
| Attorney fee provisions | Governed by contract |

**AOB Risk Assessment**: **MEDIUM** — AOBs are not prohibited, but enforceability is uncertain due to conflicting precedent. Any AOB must be carefully drafted, and policy anti-assignment clauses must be reviewed.

### 8.2 Required AOB Contract Elements (Best Practices)

While Ohio does not statutorily mandate specific AOB provisions, GCSC AOBs should include:
- Clear identification of assigned rights
- Explicit statement that assignment is post-loss
- Direction to pay contractor directly
- Scope limitation (only repair/restoration work)
- No waiver of homeowner's rights against contractor for workmanship
- Attorney fee provision (if permitted by policy)

## 9. Public Adjuster Rules

### 9.1 Ohio Public Adjuster Licensing (ORC Chapter 3951)

#### Licensing Requirements
- **Certificate of Authority** required from Ohio Department of Insurance
- Must be at least 18 years old
- Pass Ohio public insurance adjuster examination (Series 11-38)
- **$1,000 surety bond** payable to the State of Ohio
- Submit contract form to superintendent for 30-day review before use
- Application fee: $200
- Continuing education required for renewal
- **Records must be maintained for 3 years**

#### Prohibited Activities (Ohio Admin. Code 3901-1-24)

Public adjusters **CANNOT**:
1. Engage in repair/remodeling/replacement of damaged property or have any interest in a company that does so
2. Solicit during an active fire or interfere with fire investigators
3. Give any portion of their fee to an insured as inducement
4. Represent themselves as insurance company adjusters or connected to fire/law enforcement
5. Compensate unlicensed persons for solicitation, negotiation, or settlement
6. Make dishonest loss estimates
7. Have financial interest in property they adjust

### 9.2 Key Prohibitions for GCSC/Contractors

- **A contractor CANNOT act as a public adjuster** or negotiate claim settlements on behalf of a homeowner
- **A contractor CANNOT share fees with a public adjuster** in a way that violates the prohibition on compensating unlicensed persons
- **A public adjuster CANNOT have any direct or indirect interest in a repair/remodeling business**
- Only licensed public adjusters, attorneys, or court-appointed representatives may negotiate with insurance companies on behalf of insureds

### 9.3 Implications

- GCSC **must not** allow contractors to adjust claims or negotiate settlements
- GCSC **must not** share claim negotiation functions with contractors
- Any claim negotiation assistance must be provided by licensed professionals
- GCSC platform should **not** facilitate unauthorized practice of public adjusting
