## Section 1 — Executive Summary & State Overview

Texas is the **single most critical state** for SmartContractor product deployment, representing both extraordinary market opportunity and the highest conceivable regulatory risk. The Lone Star State routinely leads the nation in weather-related property insurance claims — hailstorms in the Dallas-Fort Worth Metroplex, hurricanes and tropical storms along the Gulf Coast from Houston to Corpus Christi, wind and tornado events across the Panhandle, and catastrophic freeze events like Winter Storm Uri (2021) that generated hundreds of thousands of claims. This massive claim volume creates an enormous addressable market for contractor financing and claim-advance products. However, Texas simultaneously maintains the **most aggressively anti-AOB regulatory regime in the United States outside of Florida**, and the consequences for non-compliance are uniquely severe.

### Why Texas Is the Most Important State for GCSC

- **Largest Property Insurance Claim Volume in the Continental U.S.:** Texas consistently ranks first or second nationally in annual homeowners insurance claim frequency and severity. Hailstorms in North Texas alone generate billions in losses annually. Hurricane Harvey (2017) caused $125 billion in damage. The 2024 hail season produced record claims.
- **Massive Contractor Ecosystem:** Texas has one of the largest roofing, restoration, and general contracting markets in the country, with thousands of contractors operating across Houston, DFW, San Antonio, Austin, and the Rio Grande Valley.
- **No State-Level General Contractor License:** Unlike Florida or Louisiana, Texas does not license general contractors at the state level (only HVAC, electrical, and plumbing through TDLR), creating lower barriers to entry and a larger contractor pool — but also less regulatory oversight and higher consumer protection enforcement risk.
- **Aggressive UPPA Enforcement:** Texas actively prosecutes the Unauthorized Practice of Public Adjusting (UPPA) through both criminal and civil mechanisms. The Texas Supreme Court's 2024 decision in *Stonewater Roofing v. TDI* upheld the constitutionality of Texas's UPPA statute, eliminating any constitutional challenge path for contractors.

### Five Critical Regulatory Barriers

**1. Assignment of Benefits is Functionally Dead.** Texas courts enforce anti-assignment clauses in insurance policies. Post-loss assignments of insurance claims to contractors are void as against public policy. HB 2103 (2019) explicitly prohibits all contractors from using AOB or power of attorney to circumvent insurance claim restrictions. The *Stonewater Roofing* decision (2024) forecloses any constitutional challenge.

**2. Unauthorized Practice of Public Adjusting (UPPA) is a Crime.** Under Texas Insurance Code Chapter 4102, any contractor who negotiates an insurance claim on behalf of a homeowner commits a **Class B misdemeanor** (up to 180 days imprisonment, $2,000 fine). Repeat offenses escalate to Class A misdemeanor. UPPA violations also trigger DTPA treble damages and attorney fee shifting.

**3. The 10% Constitutional Usury Ceiling.** Article XVI, Section 11 of the Texas Constitution establishes a general usury limit of 10% per annum for non-licensed lenders. The OCCC Regulated Lender license under Chapter 342 permits tiered rates up to 30% APR, but licensing is a significant undertaking requiring minimum $25,000 net assets, surety bonds, NMLS registration, and annual reporting.

**4. The 18% Prompt Payment Penalty Interest.** Texas Insurance Code Chapter 542 (the Prompt Payment of Claims Act) mandates 18% per annum simple interest on insurance claims delayed beyond 60 days after the insurer receives all required documentation. This creates enormous leverage for homeowners against insurers but also makes the claims financing space highly volatile.

**5. The DTPA — Treble Damages and Attorney Fees.** The Texas Deceptive Trade Practices-Consumer Protection Act (Business & Commerce Code Chapter 17) provides for treble (triple) damages plus attorney fees for consumer harm, including UPPA violations and misleading financing disclosures. The DTPA is one of the most plaintiff-friendly consumer protection statutes in the United States.

## Section 3 — Lending, Finance & Usury Framework

### 3.1 The 10% Constitutional Usury Ceiling

The foundation of Texas lending law is **Article XVI, Section 11 of the Texas Constitution**, which provides that any contract rate of interest exceeding **10% per annum** is usurious unless an exception applies. This is a **hard constitutional limit** that cannot be contracted around.

> **Tex. Const. Art. XVI, § 11:** "All contracts for the payment of interest upon the loan of money, or for the forbearance of the payment of money, where the rate of interest exceeds ten per cent per annum, except as otherwise provided by law, shall be deemed usurious, and no person shall recover any usurious interest."

**Consequences of Usury in Texas:**
- The usurious interest is **forfeited entirely** — the lender cannot recover any interest
- If the lender has already collected usurious interest, the borrower may recover **twice the amount of interest paid** (penalty)
- In egregious cases, the entire principal may be forfeited
- Usury is also a **defense to collection** and may be raised at any time
- **No exemption for out-of-state lenders** — Texas usury law applies to any loan made to a Texas resident or secured by Texas property

**Key Exceptions to the 10% Ceiling:**

| Exception | Statute | Maximum Rate | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| OCCC Regulated Lender — Large Loans | Tex. Fin. Code § 342.201 | Tiered up to 30% APR | Loans > $1,500; acquisition charge + monthly handling charge |
| OCCC Regulated Lender — Small Loans | Tex. Fin. Code § 342.252 | Tiered charge structure | Loans ≤ $1,500; different fee structure |
| Secondary Mortgage Loans | Tex. Fin. Code § 342.301 | Tiered up to 30% APR | Secured by 2nd lien on homestead or other real property |
| Commercial/Business-Purpose Loans | Tex. Fin. Code § 342.005 | **No limit** | Must be bona fide business purpose; documentation critical |
| Credit Union Loans | Various federal/state statutes | As permitted by charter | Federally chartered CUs preempt state usury |
| National Banks | 12 U.S.C. § 85 | Federal exportation | National banks may charge interest at rate permitted by their home state |

### 3.2 OCCC Regulated Lender License (Chapter 342)

Any entity making consumer-purpose loans exceeding 10% APR must obtain a **Regulated Lender license** from the OCCC.

**License Requirements:**

| Requirement | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Minimum Net Assets** | $25,000 (unsecured); higher if secured by real property |
| **Surety Bond** | As required by Commissioner (typically $10,000–$50,000) |
| **NMLS Registration** | Required (transitioned 2025-2026) |
| **Application Fee** | Varies by license type; typically $500–$1,500 |
| **Annual Report** | Due May 1 each year |
| **Record Retention** | 4 years from loan date or 2 years after final entry |
| **Plain Language** | Contracts must meet Flesch-Kincaid readability standards (7 TAC Ch. 90) |
| **Contract Approval** | Non-standard contract forms must be submitted to OCCC for review |

**Rate Structure for Licensed Regulated Lenders:**

| Loan Type | Maximum Charges | Statute |
|---|---|---|
| Large consumer installment loans (> $1,500) | Acquisition charge (capped) + monthly handling charge up to ~30% APR effective | Tex. Fin. Code § 342.201 |
| Small consumer loans (≤ $1,500) | Acquisition charge + monthly handling charge; effective APR varies | Tex. Fin. Code § 342.252 |
| Secondary mortgage loans | Acquisition charge + monthly handling charge up to ~30% APR | Tex. Fin. Code § 342.301 |

**Penalties for Unlicensed Consumer Lending:**
- Administrative: Cease and desist orders, fines up to $1,000 per violation
- Criminal: Willful violation is a misdemeanor
- Forfeiture of corporate charter possible for corporations
- Usurious interest forfeiture (constitutional penalty)

### 3.3 Commercial/Business-Purpose Loan Exemption

Texas Finance Code Section 342.005 provides that Chapter 342 applies only to loans "primarily for personal, family, or household purposes." **Bona fide commercial loans** to businesses are generally exempt from OCCC licensing and the 10% usury cap.

**Critical Requirements for Commercial Exemption:**
- The borrower must be a **bona fide business entity** (LLC, corporation, partnership) or sole proprietor with clear business purpose
- Loan proceeds must be used for **bona fide business purposes** (equipment, working capital, business expansion)
- **Documentation is essential:** Business tax ID, financial statements, purpose statements, invoices for business equipment/services
- **WARNING:** If a sole proprietor or small contractor uses loan proceeds for mixed personal/business purposes, the loan may be recharacterized as consumer-purpose, triggering Chapter 342 licensing requirements
- **WARNING:** Loans to individual homeowners for residential roof repair are **always consumer-purpose**, regardless of any business-purpose documentation

### 3.4 Texas Finance Code Chapter 398 — Commercial Sales-Based Financing (New 2025)

Texas enacted Chapter 398, effective 2025, creating a **registration and disclosure regime** for providers of commercial sales-based financing (revenue-based financing, merchant cash advances) to Texas businesses.

| Requirement | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Registration** | Required for providers of commercial sales-based financing |
| **Disclosure** | Specific APR-like disclosure required for transactions under $1 million |
| **Exemptions** | Traditional commercial loans, factoring, equipment financing may be exempt |
| **Penalties** | Administrative fines for non-registration or failure to disclose |

If GCSC's contractor financing product uses revenue-based repayment or sales-based structures, Chapter 398 compliance is required.

### 3.5 Debt Collection — Chapter 392

Texas Finance Code Chapter 392 regulates debt collection practices:

- **Third-party debt collectors** must be licensed and file a bond with the Texas Secretary of State
- **Debt buyers** have additional requirements under Section 392.307 (added 2019)
- **Original creditors** collecting their own debts are generally exempt from licensing but must comply with substantive restrictions (harassment prohibitions, validation requirements)
- **Smart contract automated collection** (e.g., automated liquidation of token collateral) may be characterized as "debt collection" and subject to Chapter 392 restrictions

### 3.6 Finance Charge Disclosure Requirements

All consumer loans in Texas must provide the following disclosures:

- Amount financed
- Finance charge expressed as a dollar amount
- Annual Percentage Rate (APR)
- Total of payments
- Payment schedule (number, amount, due dates)
- Late payment fees and default charges
- Prepayment policies and rebates
- Plain language requirement (Flesch-Kincaid score)
- Minimum font size requirements (7 TAC 90.103)

**FAILURE TO DISCLOSE = DTPA VIOLATION = TREBLE DAMAGES + ATTORNEY FEES**

## Section 4 — Escrow, Money Transmitter & DTPA Consumer Protection (NEW)

### 4.1 Escrow Regulation — Texas Department of Savings and Mortgage Lending

Escrow activities in Texas are regulated by the **Texas Department of Savings and Mortgage Lending (TDSML)**. Any arrangement where GCSC holds funds in trust or escrow for contractors, homeowners, or claim proceeds may trigger escrow licensing requirements.

**Escrow Agent Licensing Requirements:**

| Requirement | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Statute** | Tex. Fin. Code Ch. 158 (Escrow Officers) |
| **Regulator** | Texas Department of Savings and Mortgage Lending (TDSML) |
| **License Types** | Escrow Officer (individual), Escrow Agent (company) |
| **Net Worth** | $25,000 minimum for escrow agents |
| **Surety Bond** | $25,000–$100,000 depending on volume |
| **Experience** | 2 years relevant escrow experience for individual officers |
| **Application** | Through TDSML; background check required |
| **Examinations** | Annual examinations by TDSML |
| **Trust Account** | All escrow funds must be maintained in segregated trust accounts at Texas-chartered depositories |

**Escrow Activities That May Trigger Licensing:**
- Holding construction funds in staged disbursement (draw) accounts
- Holding insurance claim proceeds for distribution to contractors
- Holding token collateral or digital assets in escrow (uncertain — no TDSML guidance on digital asset escrow)
- Any arrangement where GCSC holds funds "in trust" for another party

**Exemptions from Escrow Licensing:**
- Attorneys holding client funds in trust accounts (IOLTA)
- Title companies holding escrow for real estate closings
- Banks and credit unions acting in fiduciary capacity
- Real estate brokers holding earnest money (subject to TREC rules, not TDSML)
- **FACTORING OF ACCOUNTS RECEIVABLE** — Purchase of accounts receivable at a discount is generally NOT escrow activity if structured as a true sale, not a loan

**⚠️ CRITICAL RISK:** If GCSC were to implement an "escrow-backed contractor advance" product — where claim proceeds are held in escrow and disbursed to contractors upon completion milestones — TDSML escrow licensing would likely be required. The absence of TDSML guidance on digital asset or smart-contract-managed escrow creates additional uncertainty.

### 4.2 Money Transmitter Licensing — Texas Finance Code Chapter 151 (MSMA)

The **Money Services Modernization Act (MSMA)**, Texas Finance Code Chapter 151, regulates money transmission in Texas. Any entity that receives money or monetary value for transmission must obtain a Money Transmitter license.

**Money Transmitter License Requirements:**

| Requirement | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Statute** | Tex. Fin. Code Ch. 151 (MSMA) |
| **Regulator** | Texas Department of Banking |
| **Minimum Net Worth** | $25,000 base; higher based on transaction volume |
| **Surety Bond** | $300,000 minimum (up to $2,000,000 based on volume) |
| **Security Device** | Surety bond, letter of credit, or additional net worth |
| **Application Fee** | $2,500 |
| **Permissible Investments** | Must maintain permissible investments equal to outstanding money transmission obligations |
| **NMLS** | Required |
| **Anti-Money Laundering** | BSA/AML compliance program required |
| **Annual Audit** | Financial statement audit required |

**Activities That Constitute "Money Transmission":**
- Receiving money or monetary value for transmission to another location or person
- Selling or issuing payment instruments (checks, money orders, stored value)
- Receiving money for obligors for bill payment services
- **Facilitating repayment of loans** through collection and remittance of funds may constitute money transmission

**Activities That Do NOT Constitute Money Transmission:**
- Pure cryptocurrency transfers (non-stablecoin) — per Supervisory Memorandum 1037
- Merchant processing (certain exemptions apply)
- Certain payroll processing services
- **True factoring** — purchase of accounts receivable is generally not money transmission

**⚠️ CRITICAL RISK FOR GCSC:** If GCSC collects loan repayments from homeowners or contractors and remits those funds to investors or funding sources, this may constitute money transmission. If repayment is routed from insurance proceeds through GCSC's control, money transmission licensing is highly likely required. Any product involving the movement of sovereign currency (USD) through GCSC-controlled accounts must be reviewed for MSMA applicability.

### 4.3 The Texas Deceptive Trade Practices-Consumer Protection Act (DTPA)

The DTPA is the **single most dangerous consumer protection statute** for GCSC in Texas. It provides extraordinary remedies to consumers and creates enormous liability exposure for any entity engaged in consumer-facing financial or insurance-related activities.

**DTPA Statutory Framework:**

| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Statute** | Tex. Bus. & Com. Code Ch. 17, Subchapter E (§§ 17.41–17.63) |
| **Enforcers** | Texas Attorney General; private plaintiffs (consumers) |
| **Covered Conduct** | False, misleading, or deceptive acts or practices in any business transaction; "unconscionable" conduct; breach of warranty |
| **Damages — Actual** | Economic damages (out-of-pocket losses) |
| **Damages — Additional** | Mental anguish damages (if defendant acted knowingly) |
| **Damages — Treble** | **Up to 3x actual damages** if defendant acted knowingly |
| **Attorney Fees** | **Mandatory fee-shifting to prevailing plaintiff** |
| **Pre-Suit Notice** | 60-day written notice required before suit; defendant has opportunity to cure |
| **Limitation Period** | 2 years from date of discovery |

**DTPA Violations Specific to GCSC Activities:**

| Activity | DTPA Risk Level | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Misrepresenting insurance claim outcomes | **EXTREME** | Promising or implying that homeowner will recover insurance funds constitutes actionable deception |
| Failing to disclose loan terms clearly | **HIGH** | Opaque or confusing smart contract terms = deceptive act |
| Misleading APR disclosure | **HIGH** | Failure to disclose true cost of borrowing violates DTPA and OCCC requirements |
| Aiding contractor UPPA violation | **HIGH** | If GCSC platform enables contractor to negotiate claims, GCSC may be liable under DTPA aiding/abetting theory |
| Token collateral liquidation without clear disclosure | **HIGH** | Homeowner must clearly understand liquidation triggers and consequences |
| Representing that GCSC is "licensed" when not | **EXTREME** | Falsely claiming OCCC or TDI licensure is per se DTPA violation |
| Bait-and-switch financing terms | **EXTREME** | Offering one set of terms and executing different terms in smart contract |
| Failing to disclose that GCSC is not a public adjuster | **HIGH** | Any confusion about GCSC's role in insurance claims = DTPA liability |

**The "Knowing" Standard:**
- **Actual Awareness:** The defendant actually knew the representation was false
- **Willful Disregard:** The defendant acted with willful disregard for the truth — no need to prove actual knowledge
- **Constructive Knowledge:** The defendant should have known — only applies to treble damages in certain circumstances

**⚠️ CRITICAL:** The DTPA does not require intent to deceive for basic liability. Even negligent misrepresentations or omissions can trigger DTPA claims. For treble damages, the plaintiff need only show "willful disregard," which is a lower standard than actual knowledge.

**DTPA Cumulative with Other Remedies:**
- DTPA claims are **cumulative** with UPPA violations, usury claims, and other statutory claims
- A single transaction can give rise to multiple causes of action, each with separate damages and attorney fee provisions
- **Example:** A contractor who uses GCSC to finance a residential roof repair and also negotiates the insurance claim faces: (1) UPPA criminal charges; (2) contract voidability; (3) DTPA treble damages; (4) usury forfeiture if interest > 10%; (5) OCCC enforcement; (6) TDI administrative penalties

### 4.4 Pre-Suit Notice and Cure Period

The DTPA requires a 60-day pre-suit notice:

1. Consumer must send written notice specifying the false, misleading, or deceptive act and the amount of damages
2. Defendant has **60 days** to make a cure offer (settlement offer)
3. If defendant makes a cure offer and plaintiff rejects it, plaintiff's damages may be limited to actual damages (no trebling) unless the final judgment exceeds the cure offer by 20%
4. Failure to provide pre-suit notice is a procedural defect that can be cured but may delay litigation

### 4.5 Texas Finance Code Chapter 392 — Debt Collection Practices

Chapter 392 supplements the DTPA with specific debt collection restrictions:

| Prohibition | Statute |
|---|---|
| Threatening violence or criminal prosecution | § 392.301(a)(1) |
| Using obscene or profane language | § 392.301(a)(2) |
| Causing expense to consumer (collect calls, telegram fees) | § 392.301(a)(3) |
| Misrepresenting the character or amount of debt | § 392.301(a)(4) |
| Communicating at unreasonable times (before 8 AM or after 9 PM) | § 392.303 |
| Communicating with consumer represented by attorney | § 392.303 |
| Harassment or repeated calls | § 392.304 |
| Failing to provide validation of debt upon request | § 392.306 |

**Smart Contract Collection Risk:** Automated liquidation of token collateral, automated repayment debits, and automated default notices may constitute "debt collection" under Chapter 392. If characterized as debt collection by a third party (GCSC collecting on behalf of a lender), licensing is required. If GCSC is the original creditor, licensing is not required but substantive Chapter 392 restrictions still apply.

## Section 5 — Contractor Licensing, UPPA & Construction Regulations

### 5.1 Texas Contractor Licensing Framework

Texas has a **dual licensing system**: state-level licensing for specific trades only, and local/municipal licensing for general contractors.

**State-Licensed Trades (TDLR):**

| Trade | License Type | Regulator | Key Requirements |

### 5.4 Unauthorized Practice of Public Adjusting (UPPA) — The Most Critical Risk

The **Unauthorized Practice of Public Adjusting** is the single greatest compliance risk for GCSC in Texas. UPPA is aggressively enforced through criminal, administrative, and civil mechanisms.

#### 5.4.1 What Is a "Public Insurance Adjuster" in Texas?

Under Texas Insurance Code Section 4102.001, a "public insurance adjuster" is any person who, for compensation:
- Acts on behalf of an insured in negotiating for or effecting the settlement of a claim
- Investigates, appraises, or evaluates claims on behalf of an insured
- Advertises or solicits business as a public adjuster

**Only three categories of people may perform these acts:**
1. A **licensed Texas public insurance adjuster** (TDI-licensed, $10,000 bond, passed exam)
2. A **licensed Texas attorney** (State Bar of Texas, in good standing)
3. **The homeowner themselves** (pro se representation)

#### 5.4.2 UPPA Prohibition — Section 4102.163

Texas Insurance Code Section 4102.163, as expanded by HB 2103 (2019), prohibits ALL contractors from:

1. **Acting as or holding oneself out as a public insurance adjuster** — Contractors cannot advertise that they will "handle your claim," "negotiate with your insurance company," or "maximize your settlement"
2. **Negotiating insurance claims on behalf of insureds** — Any discussion of claim settlement, coverage interpretation, or payment amounts with the insurer on the homeowner's behalf
3. **Using assignment of benefits or power of attorney to circumvent the prohibition** — HB 2103 explicitly added that AOB and POA cannot be used to get around UPPA
4. **Serving in a dual role as both contractor and public adjuster on the same claim** — Even licensed public adjusters cannot also be the repair contractor on the same claim

#### 5.4.3 Criminal Penalties for UPPA

| Violation | Classification | Penalty | Statute |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unauthorized public adjusting (first offense) | **Class B Misdemeanor** | Up to 180 days in county jail; $2,000 fine | Tex. Ins. Code § 4102.206 |
| Subsequent offense | **Class A Misdemeanor** | Up to 1 year in county jail; $4,000 fine | Tex. Ins. Code § 4102.206 |
| With intent to defraud | State jail felony | 180 days–2 years state jail; $10,000 fine | Tex. Penal Code § 35.02 (Insurance Fraud) |

#### 5.4.4 Civil Consequences of UPPA Violation

Beyond criminal penalties, UPPA violations trigger devastating civil consequences:

| Consequence | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Contract Voidability** | Any contract that authorizes a contractor to negotiate insurance claims is **void and unenforceable**. The contractor may recover NOTHING, even for completed work. |
| **DTPA Treble Damages** | UPPA violations are per se deceptive trade practices. Plaintiff may recover up to 3x actual damages plus attorney fees. |
| **Attorney Fee Shifting** | Prevailing plaintiff in DTPA action recovers attorney fees from defendant. |
| **TDI Administrative Action** | TDI may issue cease-and-desist orders, impose administrative fines, and revoke any applicable licenses. |
| **Restitution** | Courts may order restitution of all amounts paid by homeowner to contractor. |

#### 5.4.5 Controlling Case Law

| Case | Year | Holding | Significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| ***Stonewater Roofing v. TDI*** | 2024 (TX Supreme Court) | Upheld constitutionality of UPPA statute; regulates professional conduct, not speech; does not violate First Amendment | **DEFINITIVE** — Eliminates constitutional challenge to UPPA; TDI enforcement authority confirmed |
| ***Lon Smith & Associates v. Key*** | 2017 | Contract authorizing contractor to "pursue insured's best interest" and "reach settlement" was void as UPPA violation | Any contract language empowering contractor to negotiate is void |
| ***Wolfe's Carpet, Tile & Remodeling v. Bourelle*** | 2023 | Contract with language authorizing contractor "to negotiate to Insurance Company on our behalf" was void; contractor entitled to NOTHING for completed work | Most devastating civil consequence — complete loss of payment for work performed |

#### 5.4.6 What Contractors CAN Legally Do (Safe Harbor)

| Permitted Activity | Explanation |
|---|---|

| Discuss technical aspects of damage with adjuster | Explain scope of repair work needed |

#### 5.4.7 What Contractors CANNOT Do (UPPA Violation)

| File claim paperwork for homeowner | Completing or submitting claim forms |
| Interpret policy coverages or exclusions | Advising homeowner what is or is not covered |

## Section 6 — Insurance Claims, Prompt Payment & Penalty Interest

### 6.1 Texas Prompt Payment of Claims Act (TPPCA) — Insurance Code Chapter 542

The TPPCA is one of the most powerful consumer protection tools in Texas insurance law. It creates strict deadlines for insurers and imposes severe financial penalties for non-compliance.

**TPPCA Claim Handling Deadlines:**

| Deadline | Requirement | Statute | Consequence of Miss |
|---|---|---|---|
| **15 business days** | Acknowledge claim, begin investigation, request all needed documents | § 542.055 | Triggers subsequent deadlines |
| **15 business days** | Accept or reject claim after receiving all required items | § 542.056 | Delay starts penalty interest clock |
| **5 business days** | Pay accepted claim after notice of acceptance | § 542.057 | Delay adds to penalty interest period |
| **60 days** | If payment delayed past 60 days after receiving all docs: **18% penalty interest + attorney fees** | §§ 542.058, 542.060 | **MANDATORY 18% interest** |
| **+15 calendar days** | Extension for weather-related catastrophes (hurricanes, hailstorms) | § 542.056 | Extended timeline for catastrophe claims |

### 6.2 The 18% Penalty Interest — Section 542.060

The 18% penalty interest is **mandatory, not discretionary**, and applies regardless of the insurer's good faith:

| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Rate** | 18% per annum **simple interest** (NOT compound) |
| **Trigger** | Payment delayed more than 60 days after insurer receives all required documentation |
| **Accrual Start** | Date of violation (day 61 after documentation complete) |
| **Good Faith Irrelevant** | Even if insurer had "reasonable, good faith basis" for delay, penalty still applies |
| **Wrongful Denial = Delay** | If claim is wrongfully denied and later paid, interest runs from original due date |
| **Cumulative** | 18% penalty is cumulative with other remedies (DTPA, attorney fees, breach of contract) |
| **Forces of Nature Claims** | Chapter 542A provides alternative interest rate (~13.5% as of 2024) for hurricane/hail claims in certain counties |

**Example Calculation:**
- Claim amount: $50,000
- Insurer receives all documentation: March 1
- Payment due: April 30 (60 days later)
- Actual payment: July 1 (92 days late)
- Penalty interest: $50,000 × 18% × (92/365) = **$2,268.49**
- Plus attorney fees for TPPCA violation
- Plus potential DTPA damages
- Plus potential breach of contract damages

### 6.3 Hurricane and Hail Claims — Chapter 542A

Texas Insurance Code Chapter 542A, enacted in 2017 and amended thereafter, creates special procedures for "forces of nature" claims (hurricanes, tornadoes, hailstorms, floods):

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Pre-Suit Notice** | Claimant must provide 61-day pre-suit notice to insurer before filing suit |
| **Inspection Right** | Insurer has right to inspect property after notice |
| **Interest Rate** | Alternative interest rate (currently ~13.5%) replaces 18% for certain claims |
| **Applicability** | Applies to claims arising from "forces of nature" in designated counties |
| **Attorney Fees** | Modified fee-shifting — limited if claimant's recovery is less than certain thresholds |
| **Agent Immunity** | Insurer's agent is not liable for agent's acts unless agent is independently negligent |

### 6.4 Additional Living Expenses (ALE)

ALE coverage is standard in Texas homeowners policies:

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Typical Limit** | 10–20% of dwelling coverage (Coverage A) |
| **Time Caps** | 12–24 months common; some policies have no time cap |
| **Reimbursement Model** | Most insurers require receipts and reimburse actual expenses |
| **Advance Payments** | Insurers are NOT required to advance ALE funds but many will voluntarily |
| **Covered Expenses** | Temporary housing, additional food costs, laundry, additional transportation, pet boarding |
| **Excluded Expenses** | Mortgage payments, luxury expenses, evacuation costs (unless home uninhabitable) |
| **TWIA Policies** | Texas Windstorm Insurance Association policies require ALE endorsement (not automatic on coast) |

### 6.5 Texas Windstorm Insurance Association (TWIA)

TWIA provides windstorm and hail coverage in Texas's 14 coastal counties and parts of Harris County:

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Coverage Area** | 14 coastal counties + partial Harris County |
| **Building Code Requirement** | Structures must comply with Texas Windstorm Building Code |
| **WPI-8 Certificate** | Required for new construction and repairs in designated areas |
| **Claims Process** | Separate from standard homeowners; longer processing times typical |
| **ALE** | Must be purchased as endorsement; not automatic |
| **GCSC Relevance** | Contractors working in coastal areas frequently encounter TWIA claims; UPPA applies equally |

### 6.6 Smart Contract Implications for Claim Advances

Any GCSC product offering advances against insurance claims operates in the highest-risk regulatory space in Texas:

| Feature | Risk | Analysis |
|---|---|---|
| Advancing against anticipated claim proceeds | **EXTREME** | Effectively an assignment of claim benefits; void under Texas law |
| Repayment from insurance proceeds | **EXTREME** | Functions as de facto AOB; UPPA violation; mortgagee consent issues |
| Using 18% penalty interest as repayment source | **HIGH** | While 18% interest creates leverage, GCSC cannot be the entity that collects it |
| Accelerating payment through smart contract | **EXTREME** | Any mechanism to route claim funds to GCSC is an AOB variant |
| Claim timing prediction | **HIGH** | Predicting when insurer will pay is speculative; misrepresentation = DTPA |

### 6.7 Direction to Pay vs. Assignment of Benefits

Texas permits "direction to pay" arrangements that are distinguishable from AOB:

| Element | Direction to Pay (Permitted) | Assignment of Benefits (Prohibited) |
|---|---|---|
| **Homeowner retains claim rights** | ✅ Yes — homeowner still controls claim | ❌ No — contractor controls claim |
| **Contractor negotiates with insurer** | ❌ No — only payment direction | ✅ Yes — contractor acts as assignee |
| **Homeowner can revoke** | ✅ Yes — homeowner can change direction | ❌ No — assignment is irrevocable |
| **Insurer consent required** | ✅ Typically yes — insurer must agree | ❌ No — purportedly automatic |
| **Mortgagee involvement** | ✅ Lender may need to consent | ❌ Often bypasses mortgagee |
| **GCSC can facilitate** | ⚠️ With caution — must not cross into AOB | ❌ **NEVER** |

**⚠️ CRITICAL DISTINCTION:** The line between "direction to pay" and "assignment" is fact-specific. If GCSC's involvement creates any appearance that the contractor is controlling the claim or claim proceeds, Texas courts will likely characterize it as a prohibited assignment.

## Section 7 — Assignment of Benefits (AOB) Prohibition

### 7.1 AOB Status in Texas: **PROHIBITED / VOID / CRIMINALLY SANCTIONED**

Assignment of Benefits for post-loss insurance claims is **functionally dead in Texas**. The combination of judicial enforcement of anti-assignment clauses, statutory prohibitions, criminal penalties, and the Texas Supreme Court's *Stonewater Roofing* decision creates an absolute barrier to AOB-based products.

**This is the single most important compliance issue for GCSC in Texas.**

### 7.2 Anti-Assignment Clause Enforcement

Texas courts **routinely enforce anti-assignment clauses** in insurance policies:

- Most Texas homeowners insurance policies contain clauses prohibiting assignment of "the policy" or "any claim under the policy" without insurer consent
- Post-loss assignments of insurance claims to contractors are **void as against public policy** in Texas
- Even without an anti-assignment clause, Texas courts have held that AOB agreements in the property insurance context violate public policy when they empower contractors to negotiate claims
- The Texas Legislature codified this policy in HB 2103 (2019)

### 7.3 HB 2103 (2019) — The AOB Death Knell

HB 2103, effective September 1, 2019, amended Texas Insurance Code Section 4102.163 to explicitly prohibit AOB and power of attorney workarounds:

**Key Provisions:**

| Provision | Statute | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Expanded UPPA to ALL contractors | § 4102.163(a) | Previously targeted only roofers; now applies to every trade |
| Explicit AOB prohibition | § 4102.163(a)(2) | "A person may not use an assignment of benefits or a power of attorney to circumvent the requirements of this section" |
| Dual role prohibition | § 4102.163(b) | "A person may not serve in a dual role as both a contractor and a public insurance adjuster" |
| POA prohibition | § 4102.163(a)(2) | Power of attorney workarounds are explicitly prohibited |

### 7.4 All Attempted AOB Workarounds Fail in Texas

| Workaround | Texas Treatment | Legal Basis |
|---|---|---|
| **Assignment of Benefits** | **VOID** — anti-assignment clauses enforced; against public policy | Case law; HB 2103 |
| **Limited Power of Attorney** | **VOID** — "AOB in wolf's clothing"; explicit UPPA violation | § 4102.163(a)(2) |
| **Direction to Pay** | **PERMITTED with caution** — if homeowner retains full claim control; insurer consents; no negotiation authority transferred | Case law distinction |
| **Contingent Fee Contract** | **PERMITTED only if** contractor performs repair work ONLY and does NOT negotiate claim; percentage of repair cost (not claim amount) |
| **Invoice Factoring** | **UNCERTAIN** — True sale of accounts receivable at full recourse may be permitted; but factoring of insurance claim proceeds likely characterized as AOB | No direct Texas precedent |
| **Attorney-in-Fact Arrangement** | **VOID** — Same as POA; UPPA violation | § 4102.163(a)(2) |
| **Third-Party Claimant Status** | **VOID** — Contractor cannot become a third-party beneficiary of the insurance contract | Anti-assignment enforcement |
| **Equitable Lien on Claim Proceeds** | **LIKELY VOID** — Texas courts have rejected equitable lien theories for contractors seeking claim proceeds | Case law |

### 7.5 Direction to Pay — The Only Permissible Mechanism

A "direction to pay" is distinguishable from an AOB if ALL of the following are true:

1. ✅ The homeowner **retains full control** of the insurance claim at all times
2. ✅ The homeowner **directs the insurer** to include the contractor as a payee on the claim check
3. ✅ The contractor **does NOT negotiate** with the insurer in any capacity
4. ✅ The homeowner **can revoke the direction** at any time before payment is issued
5. ✅ The insurer **consents** to the direction (insurer is not required to honor it)
6. ✅ The mortgagee/lender **consents** if there is a loss payee on the policy
7. ✅ The arrangement is **documented as a payment convenience**, not a transfer of claim rights

**⚠️ GCSC MUST NOT:**
- Facilitate the direction-to-pay process in a way that involves GCSC in claim negotiations
- Make financing contingent on the direction to pay being honored
- Charge fees related to the direction-to-pay arrangement
- Represent that the insurer will honor the direction

### 7.6 AOB Risk Assessment: **MAXIMUM RISK**

| Risk Factor | Severity | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| **Criminal Penalty** | Class B Misdemeanor (180 days / $2,000) | UPPA criminalization applies to any claim negotiation |
| **Criminal Escalation** | Class A Misdemeanor for repeat offenses | Subsequent UPPA violations escalate |
| **Contract Voidability** | Complete loss of payment for work performed | *Wolfe's Carpet* — contractor gets NOTHING |
| **DTPA Treble Damages** | Up to 3x actual damages + attorney fees | UPPA = per se deceptive trade practice |
| **Attorney Fee Shifting** | Plaintiff recovers fees from defendant | Mandatory under DTPA |
| **TDI Administrative Action** | Fines, cease-and-desist, license revocation | Active enforcement division |
| **Insurance Fraud** | State jail felony (180 days–2 years) | If intent to defraud proven |
| **Restitution** | Full repayment to homeowner | Court-ordered disgorgement |
| **Reputational Damage** | Extreme | UPPA criminal charges destroy business viability |

### 7.7 Stonewater Roofing v. TDI (Texas Supreme Court 2024)

The *Stonewater Roofing* decision is the **definitive judicial pronouncement** on Texas AOB/UPPA law:

| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Citation** | 2024 WL 2869414 (Tex. June 7, 2024) |
| **Plaintiff** | Stonewater Roofing (contractor alleging UPPA statute was unconstitutional) |
| **Defendant** | Texas Department of Insurance |
| **Holding** | UPPA statute is **constitutional**; regulates professional conduct, not speech; does not violate First Amendment |
| **Key Reasoning** | The UPPA statute does not prohibit contractors from speaking about claims; it prohibits them from *acting* as public adjusters. The conduct-speech distinction is valid. The legislature has a legitimate interest in protecting consumers from unqualified claim negotiators. |
| **Significance** | **Eliminates any constitutional challenge to UPPA.** No contractor or platform can claim First Amendment protection for claim negotiation activities. TDI's enforcement authority is confirmed. |
| **GCSC Impact** | GCSC cannot invoke free speech arguments to justify any platform feature that facilitates claim negotiation. The *Stonewater* court made clear that Texas has the power to regulate this conduct. |

**Excerpt from the Holding:**
> "The Texas Legislature has a legitimate and substantial interest in protecting insureds from unqualified persons negotiating insurance claims on their behalf. The statute regulates professional conduct, not speech, and is therefore subject to intermediate scrutiny at most. The statute survives because it is narrowly tailored to serve the State's interest in ensuring that insurance claim negotiation is performed by qualified, licensed, and bonded professionals."

### 7.8 Lessons from UPPA Enforcement Cases

| Case | Violation | Outcome | Lesson for GCSC |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Stonewater Roofing v. TDI* | Contractor advertising "we handle your insurance claim from start to finish" | TDI cease-and-desist upheld; contractor's constitutional challenge rejected | **Do not let contractors advertise claim-handling services on platform** |
| *Lon Smith & Associates v. Key* | Contract authorized contractor to "pursue insured's best interest" and "reach settlement" | Contract void; contractor could not recover for work performed | **No contract language empowering claim negotiation** |
| *Wolfe's Carpet, Tile & Remodeling v. Bourelle* | Contract authorized contractor "to negotiate to Insurance Company on our behalf" | Contract void; contractor entitled to NOTHING despite completing work | **Even completed work goes unpaid if UPPA violation in contract** |
| TDI enforcement actions (ongoing) | Contractors acting as unlicensed public adjusters | Administrative fines, cease-and-desist orders, criminal referrals | **TDI actively enforces; not theoretical risk** |

## Section 8 — Public Adjuster & Insurance Representation Rules

### 8.1 Who May Legally Negotiate with an Insurance Company on Behalf of a Homeowner

Texas law recognizes only **three categories** of persons who may negotiate insurance claims on behalf of a homeowner:

| Category | Requirements | Limitations |
|---|---|---|
| **Licensed Texas Public Insurance Adjuster** | TDI license; $10,000 bond; passed state exam; continuing education | Cannot serve as contractor on same claim (dual role prohibition); fee capped at 10% for certain disaster claims |
| **Licensed Texas Attorney** | State Bar of Texas membership; good standing | No dual role restriction; attorney-client privilege applies; may charge contingency fees |
| **Homeowner (Pro Se)** | No licensing required | Homeowner represents themselves; no professional expertise; no fee |

**NO OTHER PERSON OR ENTITY MAY NEGOTIATE INSURANCE CLAIMS ON BEHALF OF A TEXAS HOMEOWNER.**

This explicitly includes:
- ❌ General contractors
- ❌ Roofing contractors
- ❌ Restoration contractors
- ❌ GCSC or any platform
- ❌ Third-party claim consultants
- ❌ Family members (unless attorney or licensed adjuster)
- ❌ Real estate agents
- ❌ Mortgage brokers

### 8.2 Texas Public Adjuster Licensing Requirements

| Requirement | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Statute** | Tex. Ins. Code Ch. 4102; 19 TAC Ch. 708 |
| **Regulator** | Texas Department of Insurance (TDI) |
| **Pre-Application** | Must pass state licensing exam BEFORE applying |
| **Application Fee** | $50 (resident or non-resident) |
| **Surety Bond** | $10,000 (sole principal); higher for agencies |
| **Background Check** | Fingerprints required; criminal history review |
| **Contract Requirements** | Must use TDI-approved contract (Form FIN535) or custom contract meeting 19 TAC 708 requirements |
| **Name Restrictions** | TDI does NOT accept DBA names for public adjusters |
| **Renewal** | Annual renewal required; continuing education |
| **Fee Caps** | Maximum 10% of claim settlement for certain disaster-area claims |
| **Dual Role Prohibition** | Cannot serve as contractor AND adjuster on same claim |

### 8.3 Prohibited Conduct for Licensed Public Adjusters

Even licensed public adjusters face significant restrictions:

| Prohibition | Statute | Penalty |
|---|---|---|

| Solicitation during active loss event (certain restrictions) | 19 TAC 708 | Administrative fines |

| Failing to use approved contract | 19 TAC 708.3 | Administrative action |
| Misrepresenting licensure status | § 4102.163 | Criminal charges |
| Sharing fees with unlicensed persons | § 4102.159 | License revocation |

### 8.4 Attorney Representation in Insurance Claims

Texas attorneys have broader authority than public adjusters:

- May negotiate claims without adjuster license (attorney exemption)
- Attorney-client privilege protects communications
- May charge contingency fees (typically 33-40% of recovery)
- May litigate bad faith claims (public adjusters cannot file suit)
- May serve as both attorney and contractor advisor (but not as the actual repair contractor)

**GCSC Implication:** GCSC may facilitate referrals to licensed Texas attorneys for homeowners needing claim assistance, provided:
- The referral is to a licensed attorney (verified State Bar membership)
- GCSC does not receive referral fees (unless compliant with attorney fee-sharing rules)
- The referral is clearly optional and not a condition of any financing
- GCSC does not endorse or guarantee any attorney's services

## Section 9 — Token Collateral, Digital Assets & Crypto Regulations

### 9.1 Texas Department of Banking Policy — Supervisory Memorandum 1037

The primary guidance on virtual currency in Texas is **Supervisory Memorandum 1037**, revised January 28, 2025, issued by the Texas Department of Banking.

**Key Determinations:**

| Issue | Determination | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| Is cryptocurrency "money" under Texas law? | **NO** — Non-stablecoin virtual currency is NOT "money or monetary value" under the MSMA | Pure crypto transfers are NOT money transmission |
| Is cryptocurrency a "claim"? | **NO** — Cryptocurrency does not entitle the holder to anything enforceable | No duty to honor value; not a security per se |
| Crypto-to-sovereign-currency exchange (direct, two-party) | **NOT money transmission** | Person-to-person Bitcoin sale for cash = not regulated |
| Crypto transfer by itself | **NOT money transmission** | Moving Bitcoin from wallet A to wallet B = not regulated |
| Receiving crypto for transmission alone | **NOT money transmission** | Custodial wallet services for crypto only = not regulated |

### 9.2 When Money Transmission IS Triggered

| Activity | Money Transmission? | License Required? |
|---|---|---|
| Exchange of crypto for USD **through a third-party intermediary/exchanger** | **YES** | Money Transmitter License (Ch. 151) |
| **Stablecoins** pegged to USD with redemption rights | **YES** — stablecoins ARE "money or monetary value" | Money Transmitter License |
| Transaction involving sovereign currency where GCSC controls funds | **LIKELY YES** | Money Transmitter License |
| Digital asset service provider (platform facilitating trading + custody) | **MAYBE** | Texas Finance Code Ch. 160 may apply |
| Lending against crypto collateral where loan is in USD | **UNCERTAIN** | OCCC license likely; MT license possible |

### 9.3 Texas Finance Code Chapter 160 — Digital Asset Service Providers

Chapter 160, enacted in 2023, creates a regulatory framework for "digital asset service providers":

| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Covered Entities** | Platforms that facilitate trading, exchange, or custody of digital assets for Texas residents |
| **Registration** | Required with Texas Department of Banking |
| **Consumer Disclosures** | Required — risk disclosures, fee schedules, insurance status |
| **Custody Requirements** | Segregation of customer assets; trust account requirements |
| **Anti-Fraud Provisions** | Prohibits market manipulation, deceptive practices |
| **Exemptions** | Certain banks, federally regulated entities, and low-volume providers |

**GCSC Relevance:** If GCSC maintains custody of token collateral or operates a platform where digital assets are locked, traded, or liquidated, Chapter 160 registration may be required in addition to any other licensing.

### 9.4 Token Collateral for Equipment Credit Lending

There is **no specific Texas statute** addressing cryptocurrency as collateral for loans. This creates both opportunity and risk:

| Aspect | Analysis |
|---|---|
| **Legal Validity of Crypto Collateral** | Unclear — no binding precedent in Texas; likely enforceable under general secured transaction principles if properly structured |
| **UCC Article 9 Perfection** | Token collateral may be perfected by "control" (possession of private keys) rather than traditional filing; Texas UCC Article 9 applies but smart contract "control" is untested |
| **Self-Help Repossession** | Automated smart contract liquidation may constitute "self-help repossession"; permissible under UCC § 9.609 if it can be done without breach of peace, but automated liquidation without judicial process is untested |
| **Usury Application** | If loan is denominated in USD and interest exceeds 10%, OCCC license or commercial exemption required |
| **Liquidation Notice** | Texas law requires reasonable notification of disposition of collateral (UCC § 9.611); smart contract automatic liquidation without notice may violate this requirement |
| **Commercial vs. Consumer** | Equipment credit to a bona fide contractor = likely commercial; equipment credit to a homeowner = consumer, triggering 10% usury cap and OCCC licensing |

### 9.5 Smart Contract-Specific Risks

| Feature | Risk | Analysis |
|---|---|---|
| Token collateral lock | LOW for pure crypto; HIGH if USD involved | No MT license needed for pure crypto; usury/DTPA still apply |
| Automated liquidation | **HIGH** — Self-help repossession without notice; UCC § 9.611 violation possible | Requires Texas counsel to structure compliant liquidation protocol |
| Oracle-based valuation | **MODERATE** — If oracle fails, wrongful liquidation = DTPA liability | Must have dispute resolution mechanism |
| Interest accrual in smart contract | **HIGH** — Must comply with 10% usury ceiling or OCCC licensing | Smart contract cannot override constitutional usury limit |
| Repayment routing via smart contract | **HIGH** — If USD involved, MT license likely required | Pure crypto repayment = lower risk |
| Cross-chain collateral | **UNCERTAIN** — No Texas precedent on multi-chain collateral treatment | General UCC principles likely apply |

### 9.6 Stablecoin-Specific Risk

**⚠️ CRITICAL DISTINCTION:** Supervisory Memorandum 1037 explicitly states that **stablecoins pegged to sovereign currency with redemption rights ARE "money or monetary value"** under the MSMA.

| Stablecoin Type | MSMA Treatment | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| USD-backed stablecoin with direct redemption (USDC, USDT) | **MONEY** — Money Transmitter License required | Cannot use as collateral workaround |
| Algorithmic stablecoin without redemption right | Uncertain | May fall outside MSMA; highly risky |
| Crypto-collateralized stablecoin | Uncertain | Depends on redemption mechanism |
| Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC) if issued | **MONEY** — Federal and state MSB treatment | Would be treated as sovereign currency |

**GCSC MUST NOT use stablecoins as a workaround for money transmitter or usury requirements.**

### 10.7 Texas-Specific Compliance Principles

1. **When in doubt, BLOCK.** Texas penalties are too severe to risk any non-compliant activity. Criminal liability, treble damages, and contract voidability create existential risk.

2. **Assume consumer purpose unless proven otherwise.** The 10% usury cap and OCCC licensing requirements apply broadly. Any loan to an individual, sole proprietor, or small contractor should be presumed consumer-purpose unless documented with clear, objective evidence of bona fide business use.

3. **Never facilitate claim negotiation.** UPPA is a criminal offense. GCSC must not provide any platform feature that could be characterized as facilitating insurance claim negotiation, including messaging, document management, adjuster communication, or claim strategy tools.

4. **Disclose everything.** The DTPA punishes deception and omission. Every material term must be disclosed in plain language. Every risk must be disclosed. Every limitation of GCSC's services must be disclosed prominently.

5. **Texas counsel is not optional.** The intersection of OCCC regulation, TDI enforcement, DTPA liability, constitutional usury, UPPA criminalization, and novel digital asset issues requires specialized Texas expertise. No out-of-state counsel, however experienced, can substitute for Texas-licensed practitioners.

6. **Document everything.** In a DTPA action, the defendant's documentation of good faith compliance efforts may influence damages. Maintain complete records of all disclosures, training, counsel opinions, and compliance decisions.

7. **Watch the Legislature.** Texas is actively legislating in the AOB/contractor space. New restrictions are likely in the 2027 session. Monitor bill filings and engage through trade associations.
